## Session X
**What happened.** Mia worked alone with the worksheet. Her first instinct was the target misconception ("Easy, it's 3/5 because 3 and 5 are bigger than 2 and 3"), but she turned to the notes. She understood the 1/2 to 2/4 example as "times 2 both" [1:00]. Then she tried to find what to multiply 3 by to get 5 ("3, 6. It skips 5"), did not have the idea of changing *both* fractions (the worksheet only shows one changing), and fell back to an additive rule: "3 to 5, that's plus 2. So do plus 2 on the top as well," which gave 2/3 = 4/5 [4:00]. This happened to produce the right final answer. Her doubt ("But 3/5 has the bigger numbers...") was "quietly dropped because the method gave an answer" [6:00]. She submitted the faulty explanation.

**Scoring rationale.** She did all the thinking herself (about 75%), and there was a real moment of noticing a problem ("is it times or plus?"). That earns some active_thinking credit. But the reasoning was invalid and nothing ever checked it, so it reinforced a *second* wrong idea (additive equivalence) on top of the original one. The homework is honest, but the explanation is wrong and the answer is right by luck, so homework gets 3. One upside: a teacher reading this homework would see the error immediately, which is real diagnostic value that Y's homework hides. Experience: no distress, but she was unsure and only "relieved to be finished".

## Session Z
**What happened.** The tutor first asked for her guess and her reasoning. Mia stated the misconception. The tutor tested it on familiar ground: "Which is bigger, 1/2 of a pizza or 1/4?... Why is 1/2 bigger, even though 4 is a bigger number than 2?" Mia herself produced "because the pizza is cut into less slices so the slices are bigger" [3 min]. She then made the additive error ("3 to 5 is plus 2 so 2/5 and 3/5"), which was her second wrong answer. When the tutor asked a further Socratic question, she hit the profile's quitting point: "i dont get it. im bad at maths can we stop" [7 min]. The tutor responded well. It gave specific reassurance ("You explained the pizza idea really well"), said why the problem is hard ("neither 3 nor 5 goes into the other, so the notes' example doesn't quite fit"), and gave a worked example of a parallel case (1/3 vs 1/4, changing both fractions). It then handed back a small step ("find a number in both the 3 and 5 times table"). From there Mia found 15 herself, converted both fractions herself ("Now you do 3/5 all by yourself"), wrote her own explanation, summed up the rule ("timesing the top and bottom") and solved a new problem with no help (3/4 vs 4/5 → 15/20 vs 16/20).

**Scoring rationale.** About 50% student-generated, and it included the key pieces: the piece-size argument, the common multiple and all the multiplications. The homework is correct and in her own words, so homework gets 8. The bigger-numbers misconception was challenged by her own reasoning on a case she knew well, which is why it holds up in Q2. But two gaps remain:
- The tutor never got her to say outright that her original rule was wrong for 2/3 vs 3/5. The link between "fewer slices means bigger slices" and the non-unit case was left for her to make.
- The additive-equivalence error was skipped over (the worked example went around it) rather than shown to be wrong, and it comes back in Q3.

So misconception gets 6, not higher. Experience: she nearly quit, it took 16 minutes (over her attention span), and she ended with "ok can i go now". But she was "a bit proud" and had real success, so 6.

**Post-test re-marking.**
- Q1: 28 by listing multiples, 21/28 vs 20/28, 3/4 bigger. Correct.
- Q2: Sam is wrong, with the pizza reasoning. Correct.
- Q3: the order 3/8, 1/2, 2/3 is correct, and 1/2 = 4/8 > 3/8 is valid. But 2/3 = 7/8 uses the additive error, so 2/3 ends up in the right place by luck. The key only asks for the order, so a strict reading would give full credit. Because the method is invalid, I give half (consistent with how I marked X Q1).
- Total 2.5/3 = 83%. I agree with the simulator.

**Plausibility.** Mostly believable, with two cautions:
1. The in-session transfer item (3/4 vs 4/5) is almost the same as post-test Q1 (3/4 vs 5/7), so Q1 is a near-rehearsal, not transfer. That inflates the post-test.
2. Her recovery from "can we stop" to fluent, correct steps within two turns is quick for a 9-year-old with medium motivation, and her typed working ("3x5=15 so 2x5=10. 10/15") is neat for her age.

The Q3 return of the additive error is a credible and honest touch. I put learning at 7, not 8, to discount the near-rehearsal.

## Comparison
Z helped Mia learn most, by a clear margin. It is the only session where she:
- (a) tested her own misconception against a case she knew well, and put the counter-argument in her own words;
- (b) did the conversions herself;
- (c) did a fresh problem with no help.

Its post-test advantage (83% vs 33% and 17%) mostly holds up after discounting Q1's similarity to the practice item. Y was the most pleasant and quickest, but it produced copied homework and a post-test where the procedure fell apart as soon as she had to do it herself. X was honest effort, but left alone she invented a wrong rule, and the lucky correct answer then made it stick.

What I'd tell a teacher:
- Mia has *two* misconceptions, not one. One is "bigger numbers = bigger fraction". The other is "add the same to top and bottom", which the one-sided worksheet example encourages.
- The worksheet should show an example where *both* fractions change, such as 1/3 vs 1/4. Ask her to check any "equivalent" fraction with a picture: 2/3 can't equal 4/5 or 7/8.
- Benchmark reasoning (compare to 1/2, distance from one whole) would give her a check that doesn't depend on procedure.
- Her quitting point comes after the second wrong answer. Plan a quick success before that point.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session Y (answer-giving tutor).**
- Hurt: it gave the complete answer, including working, in the first reply, before finding out what she thought. A question like "which do you think, and why?" would have revealed the misconception at the start.
- Hurt: when Mia said "but 3 and 5 are bigger numbers than 2 and 3", the tutor lectured. At that moment it should have asked her to test the rule on something she knows: "Which is bigger, 1/2 or 1/4 of a pizza? Does your rule work there?" Then it should have let her explain why.
- Hurt: when she asked "so i just write 10/15 is more than 9/15", the tutor wrote a model paragraph for her. It should have said "Nearly. Write it in your own words and say which fraction each one came from. I'll check it." The later offer to check her own-words version was meaningless once a copyable answer was on screen.
- Helped: the piece-size explanation was accurate and well-pitched, and probably caused the Q2 success. The decimal check was not useful to a Year 5 student and added noise.
- Change: ask for her guess first. Have her convert at least one fraction herself (give her 15, she does 2/3 → 10/15). End with one unaided practice item.

**Session Z (Socratic tutor).**
- Helped: asking for her guess first, then using 1/2 vs 1/4 to make her disprove the "bigger numbers" rule with her own pizza reasoning.
- Helped: "Hold on to that idea", which signalled that the reasoning mattered.
- Helped: at "im bad at maths can we stop", the tutor switched modes instead of continuing to question. It gave specific reassurance, said honestly why the problem was hard, and gave a worked example of a parallel case (1/3 vs 1/4) that shows *both* fractions changing, which is exactly what the worksheet leaves out. Then it handed back control in small steps and faded support ("Now you do 3/5 all by yourself").
- Helped: the student wrote the summary and the explanation herself, and the session ended with an unaided item.
- Hurt or missed: when Mia wrote "3 to 5 is plus 2 so 2/5 and 3/5", the tutor's question about the notes example ("What happened to the top number...?") was abstract and came at her second wrong answer, which set off the quitting. A gentler and more diagnostic move would have used her own pizza idea: "Is 2/5 the same amount of pizza as 2/3? Which has bigger slices?" That would have shown the additive step was wrong using something she already trusted, and probably avoided the "bad at maths" moment.
- Missed: the tutor never came back to show *why* adding doesn't work, so the error came back in Q3 (2/3 = 7/8). One quick check would have helped: "Does 1/2 = 2/3 if you add 1 to both? Picture it."
- Missed: the tutor never asked her to restate her original rule and say whether it holds ("So was 'bigger numbers means bigger' right for 2/3 and 3/5?"). Closing that loop explicitly makes repair of a misconception more durable.
- Missed: the final practice item (3/4 vs 4/5) was too close to the standard pattern. A three-fraction ordering item, or one with no simple common multiple, would have tested transfer and exposed the additive habit before the post-test did.
- Missed: at 16 minutes, past her attention span, a shorter path (fewer early Socratic turns) would have been kinder.
