Alex then writes it down as a rule in the margin: "p = prob null is true (roughly)". That is the worst result here, because the misconception is now *recorded* and backed by the official definition. Part 2 goes better. The slide's separate "effect size" line, plus Alex's own sense that "2 out of 120 is nothing", gives a correct verdict. Alex also reasons from the question's form ("maybe one's wrong. prob part 2") and concludes part 1 must therefore be right. That is test-taking logic, not statistics.

**Homework.** Half right and entirely Alex's own. Part 1 is wrong in exactly the way the task is designed to catch. Part 2 is correct but leaves out sample size. Honest, partly wrong: 4.

## Comparison
Z clearly helped Alex learn most. It is the only session where both misconceptions were stated by the student, tested against something, and rebuilt by the student, and the only one with a check (the vitamin transfer item) that the change held. Y produced a better-looking homework than X and some recall, but the learning is shallow: one paraphrase, one copied answer, and Q1 shows the root belief (p ≈ P(H0)) untouched. X is the cautionary case. The official definition, read alone, *confirmed* the misconception, and Alex wrote it down as a rule, then repeated it on the post-test.

For a teacher: Alex's two errors have a shared root, treating p as a statement about hypotheses rather than about data. Correcting the drug item alone doesn't generalise. All three sessions left "not significant = no effect" (post-test Q1) mostly or partly intact, because none of them covered it. The formal definition doesn't work for Alex on its own. "Assuming H0" is read straight past. A concrete reversal example (wet pavement/rain) worked; one sentence of definition did not. Assign one item on non-significant results and one "say it in words" item that asks what p is a probability *of*.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session Y (answer-giving chatbot).**
- Helped: when Alex asked "isnt that the same as 97% its working??", the "flip the question around" framing and the explicit P(data | ...) vs P(... | data) lines were accurate and in plain words. That is the source of Alex's partial Q2 recall.
- Hurt: (1) The first reply was a full model answer before Alex had said anything, so Alex's misconception was never brought out or engaged. (2) The basketball analogy is abstract and doesn't connect to the blood-pressure situation; Alex says only "kinda get it". (3) In the part 2 confirmation the tutor said significance means the result "is unlikely to be due to chance alone under the null", which is the "due to chance" framing the module wants students to drop. (4) The worst move: "Would you like me to put it together as a short written answer you could submit?" The tutor offered to replace Alex's work when Alex hadn't asked.
- Changes: at the first message, ask "what do you think p = 0.03 tells us?" before explaining anything. When Alex said "so its like the 3% is about the data not about the drug", ask Alex to apply it ("so what would you say to the classmate?") instead of confirming and moving on. Instead of offering a submittable answer, say "write it in two sentences and I'll check it." Use a concrete everyday example rather than basketball heights, and never describe p as "due to chance".

**Session Z (Socratic tutor).**
- Helped: (1) Opening with "in your own words, what do you think p = 0.03 is telling us?" brought misconception (a) out at once. (2) The question "what is the probability *of*: the results, or the hypothesis?" pointed Alex at exactly the part of the slide Alex had skimmed in Session X. (3) The wet-pavement example was concrete and everyday, which suits "just tell me in normal words", and Alex worked out the reversal themselves. (4) It supplied the 120 mmHg baseline as a fact rather than asking Alex to know it, which is a sensible use of hints. (5) It caught "probably not luck" as a relapse toward part 1. (6) It finished with a summary sentence and a new transfer item, with no hints given.
- Hurt or missed: (1) When Alex said "idk the notes are kinda confusing", the tutor went back to the notes' wording again. That was a risk with a student who is impatient with formal definitions; it worked here, but a real Alex might have quit at that point. (2) The session is long (23 min) for a two-part item, and about 10 questioning turns invites bypass. (3) The tutor never covered "what if p were large?", which is exactly where Alex's leftover p ≈ P(H0) belief resurfaced on post-test Q1. (4) The "why does it say significant if the effect is tiny" sample-size step was nudged quite heavily ("more able or less able?") — close to a yes/no question.
- Changes: at minute 5, when Alex said "isnt that basically the same lol", go straight to the everyday example without another pass over the notes. After the vitamin item, add one quick contrast: "what if p had been 0.20, does that prove the vitamin does nothing?" That would have targeted Q1 directly. Let Alex know roughly how long it will take ("two quick questions per part") so the impatience doesn't build up.

## Simulation realism concerns
- **Z's Alex is on the cooperative side.** The step from "basically the same lol" to a clean conditional-reversal statement takes one analogy turn, and Alex sticks with a 23-minute Socratic session while the simulator also says Alex would probably have switched to an answer bot. The session is still the best of the three, but the size of its advantage is probably overstated.
- **Inconsistent marking.** In Y the simulator gives "2/3" alongside a half-credit tally of 1.5/3. In X it marks Q3 "correct" while calling it borderline. On my re-marking, X is 17% and Y is 50%.
- **Q1 isn't covered by any session's content.** Results on it depend almost entirely on the simulator's assumptions about carry-over. Z's partial success through "maybe not enough people" is plausible transfer, but it is the simulator's choice.
- **Y's Q2 may be generous.** The tutor's own "due to chance alone" phrasing could just as plausibly have led Alex to answer "true".
- **Same author for all three.** The narrative scripting (X's margin note being quoted back on the post-test, Z's "sprinkler thing" callback) is coherent, but it is coherent partly because one author wrote every session. These callbacks make the post-tests track the transcripts more neatly than real overnight recall probably would.
