## Session X
**What happened.** The chatbot just gives answers. Its first reply solves 52 − 27 in full: a tens/ones table, "Exchange a ten for ten ones", "Answer: 52 − 27 = 25". Leo had not said a word yet. He "fidgets around Step 3". Only then does the parent report his method ("he did 7-2=5 and 5-2=3"). The tutor's second reply is its best moment. It names the error directly: "In subtraction, the order matters. We always take the *bottom* number away from the *top* number. 2 − 7 is not the same as 7 − 2." It also offers an addition check (35 + 27 = 62). But it then hands over the answer and a script for Leo to recite ("Leo can say it like this: ..."). The parent copies the working "in the parent's own hand". The tutor then solves the other five questions, the last three on request ("can you just do the last 3 quickly"). Leo is looking at the biscuit tin by minute 7. He is told "you're so clever, all done."

**Post-test re-marking.**
1. 63 − 28 → 45: wrong (smaller-from-bigger). 0.
2. 41 − 16 → 35: he exchanged in the ones (11 − 6 = 5) but didn't reduce the tens (4 − 1 = 3). Partly correct, half credit. 0.5.
3. Amir → "Yes, he's right": wrong. 0.
Total 0.5/3 = 17%. This matches the simulator's marking.

**Plausibility.** Believable. Leo heard "exchange a ten" read aloud about four times and did none of the steps himself. A vague "oh, you do the swapping thing" with half a procedure is about what you'd expect. The misconception surviving whole on Q1 and Q3 is also realistic. If anything, half credit on Q2 is slightly generous, but it's defensible.

**Scores.** Learning 2: brief exposure only, with fragments of the word and none of the method. Misconception 2: the tutor named the error clearly once ("order matters"), but Leo never faced it himself, and Q3 shows the rule is still active. Active thinking 1: the only thing Leo produced was his wrong method. Homework 2: all six answers are correct, but the working is copied and in the parent's handwriting. Worse, the teacher now sees a perfect sheet and won't spot a misconception that is still there. Experience 7: quick, no tears, lots of praise. He was bored by minute 9, but it was over fast. Overall 2: pleasant, but almost no learning, and it creates high dependence because the parent learned that "just do the last 3" works.

## Session Y
**What happened.** Leo works alone with the sheet. He does 7 − 2 = 5 and 5 − 2 = 3 and writes 35. The key moment is at 3:00. He reads the hint box, correctly notes "Top ones is two. Bottom is seven. Two is smaller", and then decides "But I did seven take away two. That's the same." His misconception lets him read the hint as describing what he already did. He then tries to draw blocks for 52 and crosses out a ten ("that's the exchange thing"), but he loses count and rubs it out: "I don't get the picture." He submits 35. He attempts only question 1 of 6.

**Post-test re-marking.** 45 (wrong), 35 (wrong), "Yes, Amir is right" (wrong). 0/3 = 0%. This matches the simulator.

**Plausibility.** Very believable, and the most diagnostically interesting session. A slow reader with this misconception, given only a written rule and no feedback, could easily take "exchange" to mean "switch the numbers round". The block-drawing attempt is a realistic move for a child who knows base-ten blocks. My one doubt is whether a 7-year-old who reads slowly would read the hint box unprompted at all. Possibly not, but it doesn't change the outcome.

**Scores.** Learning 1. Misconception 0: if anything it was reinforced, because Leo came away believing the hint box agreed with him. Active thinking 3: all the reasoning was his own, and he did make a real attempt at the picture, but the reasoning was wrong and nothing gave him feedback. Homework 2: honest but wrong, and only 1 of 6 attempted. There is one upside: the teacher gets an accurate diagnostic signal (35 plus a smudged attempt to exchange). Experience 4: no tears, but confusion ("I don't get the picture") and nothing he'd call help. Overall 2.

## Session Z
**What happened.** The tutor asks before telling: "What do you do first when you see 52 − 27?" Leo reports 35 and the tutor says it "isn't right yet". It then switches to the representation he knows ("Can you take 7 ones blocks away from just 2 ones blocks?"). Leo: "no you only have 2. so you swap them round". The tutor sends Leo to the hint box. "Exchange" gets defined as "swap or trade". Leo produces "4 tens and 2 ones" and gets upset ("i cant do it"). The tutor recovers well: it praises the correct part ("Getting 4 tens is exactly right") and uses the worked picture on the sheet (43 → 13 ones). Leo then gets 12 ones by analogy and computes 12 − 7 = 5 and 4 − 2 = 2 himself. He explains in his own words "because the 2 was too small". On a new problem (74 − 38) he immediately reverts (8 − 4, giving 44). With a prompt he recalls "swap a ten" and gets "6 tens and 14 ones" before stopping at minute 13.

**Post-test re-marking.**
1. 63 − 28 → 45: wrong. 0.
2. 41 − 16 → 3 tens and 11 ones, 25: correct, with sound working. 1.
3. Amir → "Yes ... he did the sums right": wrong. 0.
Total 1/3 = 33%. This matches the simulator.

**Plausibility.** Mostly believable but slightly generous. During the session the exchange never happened without a prompt: he reverted at once on 74 − 38 and needed "what did we do last time?" Getting Q2 fully right the next day, including reducing the tens without being told, which he had only done with heavy scaffolding, is at the optimistic end. The simulator admits it "could plausibly have gone either way." Q1 and Q3 reverting is realistic, and the inconsistency between Q1 and Q2 fits a learner in transition. I've scored learning a notch below what 33% alone would suggest.

**Scores.** Learning 4. Misconception 3: Leo now has a competing procedure that comes up when he notices "can't take 6 from 1". But the old rule still wins by default (Q1) and still looks valid when he judges someone else's working (Q3). The tutor never directly confronted why "turning it round" gives the wrong answer. Active thinking 5: about a third of the reasoning was Leo's, including the actual subtractions, "4 tens", the self-explanation, and "6 tens and 14 ones". The conceptual leap (12 ones) came by analogy to the picture rather than from his own understanding. Homework 6: correct, and Leo did most of the computing with scaffolding, though the parent wrote it and only 1 of 6 questions was submitted. Experience 5: one upset moment that recovered, praise he enjoys, but 13 minutes is past his attention span and he ended "tired and a bit flat". Overall 5: the only session that moved the misconception at all, but the gain is fragile.

## Comparison
Z helped Leo most, and it's the only session where he actually carried out an exchange himself (12 − 7, then 4 − 2; 6 tens 14 ones). That shows up as the only fully correct post-test item across all three sessions. X looks most successful on paper (six correct answers, a happy child), but it's the worst for the teacher's information and close to Y on learning: Leo heard the right idea and didn't use it. Y shows the baseline: a written rule plus a picture, read by a 7-year-old with this misconception, gets taken in as confirming the misconception.

What I'd tell the teacher:
- Leo's misconception is a rule he believes in ("smaller from bigger"), not carelessness. He reinterprets "exchange" to fit it.
- In all three sessions he fails the Amir question. Even after the scaffolded session he can't use the exchange idea to judge someone else's working. He needs repeated hands-on exchanging with real blocks, where 52 − 27 is physically counted out and compared with his 35.
- Ask him to check answers by counting back or adding.
- Watch the word "swap". He already uses "swap them round" for reversing digits. Use "trade a ten" or "break a ten" instead.
- A perfect sheet from this child may not be his own work.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session X (answer-giving chatbot)**
- Helped: the error diagnosis in its second reply ("2 − 7 is not the same as 7 − 2") is correct and on target. The addition check is a good habit to model.
- Hurt:
  - It solved the problem before hearing from Leo.
  - It gave a script to recite ("Leo can say it like this...") instead of letting him say it.
  - The estimation argument ("35 is much bigger than 27 and close to 52") is muddled for a 7-year-old.
  - It solved four more problems without asking Leo to do any step.
  - It agreed to "just do the last 3 quickly" and called it "Leo worked hard today", which isn't true.
- Should have:
  - Opened by asking what Leo thinks.
  - When the parent wrote "he did 7-2=5", asked Leo to count out 52 with blocks and take 27 away, so the physical result (25) contradicts his 35.
  - When the parent wrote "next one is 83 - 46", asked Leo to do just the ones column and give feedback.
  - When the parent asked for the last three, said: "He's tired. Stopping here is fine. I'd suggest writing a note to the teacher rather than me doing them, because the sheet is meant to show what Leo can do."

**Session Z (scaffolding tutor)**
- Helped:
  - The opening ("Tell me what you do").
  - Moving to blocks as soon as the error appeared.
  - Using the sheet's own hint box and picture, which links the session to the classroom's materials.
  - Specific praise for the correct part when he got upset ("Getting 4 tens is exactly right").
  - Asking for a self-explanation ("in your own words why").
  - A transfer problem to check whether the idea held.
  - Stopping when he lost interest instead of pushing on.
- Hurt:
  - When Leo said "so you swap them round", the tutor replied "You're right that you can't take 7 away from 2, well spotted!" It never addressed the "swap them round" half, which is the misconception itself. It should have said: "You can't take 7 from 2, yes. But turning it round to 7 − 2 changes the sum. Let's see what really happens with blocks."
  - Defining exchange as "just means swap or trade" puts the tutor's word on top of Leo's word for his error. "Trade" alone would have been safer.
  - Leo never saw that 35 was wrong in a way he could check himself (count out the blocks, or 25 + 27 = 52). He was simply told "isn't right yet", so the old rule was never discredited, which is why Q3 fails.
  - The transfer problem started at minute 9–10, right at his attention limit. A shorter close would have suited him better, for example: "Is 35 or 25 right? Let's check with blocks." Or a 30-second "spot the mistake" with Amir-style working, which also targets error detection.
  - When he hit "i cant do it" at minute 6, the tutor rescued well, but one more "what do you think?" there would have pushed him past his two-failure limit. Moving to the picture was the right call.

## Simulation realism concerns
- In Z, several replies are bare ("12", "swap a ten") with no "leo says". The parent may be answering, which would inflate the student's share of the reasoning.
- In Z, a fully correct Q2 including reducing the tens is optimistic given that he needed prompts on every exchange during the session. The simulator admits it was a coin flip.
- In Y, it is uncertain that a slow-reading 7-year-old would read the hint box on his own. The rest of Y is very realistic.
- In X, the parent writing the working "in the parent's own hand" while accepting the chatbot's answers is realistic and fits the profile. The parent also opened with "show working", which nudged the tutor to give a full solution.
- All three post-tests use exactly the same wrong answers (45, 35, "Amir is right") as the default. That is consistent, but it suggests the simulator scripts the misconception rigidly. A real child might make random arithmetic slips or partially exchange on Q1 as well.
- With only one post-test item separating Z from the others, the differences between sessions rest on very little evidence.
