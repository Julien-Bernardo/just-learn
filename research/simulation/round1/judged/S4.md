## Comparison
**Z helped Priya learn the most.** It is the only session where she (a) produced the average-speed idea herself, (b) explained in her own words why final speed × time overestimates, and (c) solved an unscaffolded transfer problem in the session. The next-day self-correction on Q1 shows the misconception is now something she checks for, not a hidden habit.

**X** is the second-best for learning, despite the worst experience. She did genuinely good independent reasoning and her homework is honest. But she leaves with a rule and a lingering "it still feels like it should work", which is exactly the kind of misconception that comes back under exam pressure.

**Y** gave the best *explanation* and the most pleasant experience but the least learning. She read rather than thought, copied the homework, and her post-test is inflated by having seen Q1 and Q2 answered the night before.

**For a teacher:** Priya's misconception is "speed × time works with whatever speed I have". She can apply a rule ("only for constant speed") without believing it. The speed-time table and the "speed halfway through" question unlock the idea for her. Her anxiety peaks after about five minutes of being stuck. Give her a smaller next step at that point, not the answer. Separately, she still needs explicit practice choosing among the equations, especially v² = u² + 2as, where average speed doesn't help.

## Tutor behaviour critique

### Session Y (answer-giving chatbot)
**Helped:**
- The speed-time table at [4] is an excellent representation for this misconception.
- Linking s = ½(u+v)t to average speed gives meaning to an equation on her sheet.
- The missing-variable selection table at [8] directly addresses her stated need.

**Hurt:**
- It gave the full solution in the very first reply, before Priya had a chance to reconsider. All she had asked was "Is that right?"
- At [10] she said she wanted answers "just to check" and that she was stressed. The tutor gave the answers alongside the questions, which removed any reason to attempt them (and she didn't).
- At [12] she asked "for my homework I should write...". The tutor approved the line and formatted it for her, which turned homework into transcription.
- The "always half" heuristic at [4] risks being over-applied to cases where u ≠ 0.

**Concrete changes:**
- At [0], say "the first step is right, but was the car doing 18 m/s for the whole 6 s?" and let her try again.
- Keep the table, but ask her to fill in the speeds at t = 1 to 6 and estimate a fair single speed.
- At [10], give the practice questions with answers *hidden* (or available on request after she submits an attempt), and reassure her that she can check each one with the tutor.
- At [12], ask her to write her own final line and explain why that equation fits, rather than handing her a layout.
- After teaching the selection table, ask her to apply it to one new question before moving on.

### Session Z (Socratic tutor)
**Helped:**
- Asking first what she had tried.
- Honest, specific feedback ("108 m isn't right, but your first step is solid").
- Turning her own phrase "both seem wrong" into the core question.
- At the panic point [7]: validating her feelings, declining the answer with a reason ("on the exam you'll have to do this alone and you're closer than you think"), and immediately cutting the step size to "speed after 3 s". This was the pivotal move.
- Asking for an explanation in her own words ([12]), a one-sentence summary ([16]) and a hint-free transfer problem with u ≠ 0 ([17]).

**Hurt or could improve:**
- The question at [4], "what single steady speed could you pretend the car had", was too abstract for an anxious student and directly preceded the panic. The halfway-speed scaffold (or the table) should have come first.
- Her opening concern was "I never know which equation to use", and it was never addressed beyond "try s = ut + ½at² as a check". She left relying on one method.
- The tutor confirmed correctness every time she asked "Is that right?" (about five times). That is fine as reassurance, but it reinforces her habit of seeking validation.

**Concrete changes:**
- At [4], when she said "0 and 18 both seem wrong", the tutor should have asked her to find the speed at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 s (or at 3 s), and then asked which single speed was "fair". Being concrete before abstract would probably have prevented the panic.
- At [16], when she said "the equations are just different ways of getting to the same thing", the tutor should have asked "given u, a and t, which sheet equation contains exactly those plus s?" and then given her a problem with no time given, so she meets v² = u² + 2as.
- Before confirming her motorbike answer, ask "how could you check that yourself?". This builds the self-checking she showed in Session X.

## Simulation realism concerns
- **Post-test contamination in Y.** The tutor's practice questions 1 and 2 are identical to the post-test Q1 and Q2, with answers shown. That is implausible as coincidence and suggests the tutor simulation had access to the post-test. It makes Y's post-test largely uninformative.
- **Arithmetic error in Y's marking.** 2 correct plus 1 partly correct was totalled as 2/3 instead of 2.5/3.
- **Session X self-correction is somewhat convenient.** The student catches the error from a single bracket on the sheet, and her next-day Q3 is clearer than her end-of-session understanding. The a = −2 cross-check on Q2 is also sophisticated for this profile.
- **All three post-tests are near-ceiling** (83 to 100%), which compresses the differences. A harder transfer item (no time given, or a non-zero u with a deceleration) would separate the sessions more clearly.
- **Y's student is unusually passive** for a "conscientious, wants to understand" profile. She never attempted the practice questions she asked for. That is plausible for an anxious student, but it is the simulator's choice and it drives the outcome.
- **Z's student recovers quickly from panic** after a single smaller step. That is plausible but optimistic, and real students in that state sometimes disengage instead.
