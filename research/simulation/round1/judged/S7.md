## Comparison
X helped Sam learn most. X and Z have the same re-marked post-test score, but they differ in the quality and durability of what sits behind it. In X, every fix was justified by a specific trace or counterexample. Sam can say why None is the right starting value and why `!=` is needed, and his solution is the clean canonical one, which will transfer to second_smallest, k-th largest and similar problems. In Z, Sam discovered tracing for himself, and that is valuable and arguably "stickier" as a habit, because he saw that it worked when nothing else did. But he also got through by patching, ended with code he doesn't fully understand, and spent 8 minutes flailing. Y was by far the worst for learning. It was the most pleasant and fastest, and it produced correct homework, but there was zero student reasoning, and the next-day function fails the core "distinct" requirement.

For a teacher: Sam's real problem is not Python. It is that he debugs by perturbation. When he traces, he finds bugs quickly and accurately (he did so in both X and Z within a minute or two). The intervention he needs is a forcing rule, such as "no rerun until you've written a predicted trace". It should also stress the "answer appears first" class of tests: no version of Sam produced one in Q2, even though in X he had just traced exactly such a case (`[3,9,9,9]`). He needs explicit instruction in deriving test cases from how the code can fail (the position of the answer, the initial value), not from a memorised list.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session X (Socratic tutor).**
- Helped: asking "what have you tried so far" surfaced the guess-and-check habit, and the tutor named it without lecturing. Pointing to his own notes rather than to the tutor's authority helped. The `[-3,-8,-5]` counterexample at minute 15 was excellent: it let Sam reject 0 and arrive at None himself. The prompts at minute 22 and 25 that sent him to trace `[5,5,3]` rather than explaining the bug worked well. The one-sentence summary request and the `[3,9,9,9]` transfer probe were both good.
- Hurt or missed: at minute 9 the tutor said "run [5, 1, 4] by hand again before you run it on the computer. What do you predict?" Sam ignored this ("i added it and ran it") and the tutor let it pass. That was the exact moment to reinforce prediction before execution. It should have said something like "you ran it first — what would the trace have told you?" At minute 22 the tutor told him to trace. Better would have been "what would you do now?", to see whether Sam picks tracing himself. That is the real test of whether the habit moved. The closing "list the test cases you'd use and why" was left as homework. Spending 2 more minutes on it in-session would have targeted his weakest area (Q2) directly. Asking "in which position in the list was the answer in `[3,9,9,9]`, and why might that break code?" would have covered the gap the post-test exposed.

**Session Y (answer-giving assistant).**
- Helped: the diagnosis was accurate, the explanation mapped each bug to each failure, and the trace offer was a good instinct.
- Hurt: it handed over complete working code in the first reply, which made everything after it optional. When Sam asked "so i just need to start with None?" he showed partial understanding, and that was a teachable moment. The tutor answered the question for him. It should have asked "what would `[5,5,3]` return if you only changed that? trace it". The line "If you paste the version you end up with, I can check it" invited copy-paste. The tables of checks gave him a test list to parrot (visible in Y's Q2) instead of a method. When Sam declined the tracing explanation, the tutor accepted immediately. Better would have been a 60-second micro-task, such as "before you submit, tell me in one line why `n != largest` is there", which would likely have kept the line from being dropped in his post-test. Concretely: lead with the diagnosis of the first failure only, ask him to trace it, and withhold the full code unless he is truly stuck or out of time. With 2 hours left, he was not out of time.

**Session Z:** no tutor (notes only). The notes' tracing instruction was the single most effective intervention in this session. It just arrived 8 minutes late because nothing prompted Sam to read it.
