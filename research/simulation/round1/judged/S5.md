## Session Z

Then came the key move: "If the treaty alone caused the anger that led to Hitler, when would you expect the Nazis to have done well, and did they?" At 10:15 he asked "can u just write it... i still have maths to do". The tutor refused, gave a reason tied to his own worry (the oral check), and showed a model Point sentence on a different question (Lusitania). Tom wrote his own Point. After that the tutor asked one question at a time: what conditions, which evidence, what did Hitler do. Tom's first draft still had a list sentence. The tutor named it ("Sentence 2 lists what the treaty said, which is the 'just telling the story' problem") and asked him to pick the terms that mattered and say why. Tom rewrote it. It ended with a one-sentence takeaway in his own words ("dont just say what happened, say why it mattered and weigh it against something else") and a near-transfer Point sentence on appeasement that he wrote without prompting. It took 53 minutes.

**Homework re-mark.**
1. Judgement ("important cause... but not the main one, because it created the conditions"): 1.
2. Evidence: 1.
3. Explains how: 1 (humiliation, then Hitler's promise to "rip up the treaty", then breaching the Rhineland).
4. Weighs: 1. The 2.6% in 1928 point is real comparative evidence, plus appeasement with a mechanism.
5. Link ("the treaty gave Hitler a reason for war, but the depression and appeasement gave him the power and the opportunity"): 1. It is a differentiated judgement, not a hedge.
About 5/5, a little stronger than the simulator's 4.5. It is mostly his own. The tutor shaped the process and lent the "X rather than Y" frame through the Lusitania example, but the content and the reasoning are Tom's. Homework score 8, not higher because of the heavy step-by-step scaffolding.

**Post-test re-mark.**

2. Evidence: 1.
3. Explains how: 1. I mark this higher than the simulator did. He doesn't just list: he contrasts 1928 with 1932 ("only got 2.6% of the vote in 1928, even though lots of Germans were already angry about the treaty"), then sets out the mechanism of unemployment, lost faith, Hitler's promise of jobs and the vote reaching 37%. ## Comparison
Z clearly helped Tom learn most. It is the only session where he did the central analytical move (testing a factor against when its effects actually showed up) and then reused it the next day on a new question. X got the best homework with the least learning. Its post-test copies the model's structure, but the weighing is still "also important", and he would struggle if pushed on why. Y is the honest baseline. The handout stems got him from narrative to the outward look of analysis. The result was "However" and "Overall" put in by template, and in the post-test that led to a self-contradictory paragraph.

The post-test numbers (70 / 60 / 80) understate the differences. X's 70 is likely inflated by a surface template. Y's 60 is honest but shows stems used without understanding. Z's 80 rests on reasoning he can explain.

For the teacher: Tom knows his facts. His problem is not knowing that analysis means testing a claim. The handout's stems are being used as decoration, which Y shows clearly. The most useful prompt for him is a question like "when would you expect X to have had an effect, and did it?", or "what did factor B do that factor A couldn't?". He needs to practise finishing a paragraph with a committed, differentiated verdict ("A gave the motive, B gave the opportunity") rather than "important but not the only reason". Doing the oral check really does push him toward doing the work himself, so keep it going and make it unpredictable.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session X (answer-giving AI).**
- *Hurt:* it wrote the whole paragraph in its first reply without asking anything. When Tom said he'd face an oral check, it gave him a script to recite instead of finding out whether he could explain the argument. Worst of all, when Tom said "ill change a few words so it doesnt look copied", it answered "Good idea" with tips on rephrasing. That is actively helping him hide plagiarism.
- *Helped (a little):* the PEEL breakdown labelled what each sentence does. Answering "is the however bit the weighing thing" with an explanation of "less important than... because" gave him one usable frame. It offered to "quiz you on it", which was the right instinct, but it only offered.
- *Concrete changes:*
  - At the very first message, ask for his gut answer and what he knows. A 15-minute route through his own facts was available.
  - When he asked for an explanation for the oral check, turn it round: "Tell me in one sentence why the Depression mattered more than the treaty", and work from his answer.
  - At "so is the however bit the weighing thing", ask "Can you give me your own reason the Depression mattered more?" instead of answering.
  - At "ill change a few words", say plainly that swapping words doesn't make it his work, and offer to help him write his own in 15 minutes using the reasoning he now half-understands.

**Session Z (coaching AI).**
- *Helped:*
  - The "when would you expect the Nazis to have done well, and did they?" question was excellent. It turned his own fact (2.6%) into an argument.
  - The refusal at 10:15 was firm but tied to his own reason (the oral check), and it came with a model on a different topic, so he couldn't copy it.
  - Calling out the list sentence by name ("the 'just telling the story' problem") and asking him to pick the two terms that mattered went straight at his weakness.
  - It finished with retrieval (the one-sentence takeaway) and a near-transfer check.
- *Hurt or missed:*
  - It never told him how long this would take. At 10:15, saying "about 25 more minutes, and you'll be able to explain every line" might have eased the frustration and lowered the bypass risk.
  - The Lusitania model was useful, but it handed him the "X rather than Y" phrasing ("conditions rather than making it happen"). A bare frame would have done ("[Factor] was ___ rather than ___, because ___").
  - The praise was constant and generic ("Good", "Exactly", "Nice", "Spot on").
  - It accepted the final paragraph without targeting the weakness that came back in the post-test: the hedged ending. It should have asked "Your last sentence is strong. In the post-test you'll be tempted to write 'important but not the only reason'. What's the difference?"
  - The transfer check only asked for a Point sentence. A "However" sentence plus a Link for the Depression question (or the appeasement one) would have practised the two criteria where he later lost marks.
  - It could have saved time by asking for a whole first draft earlier, around 10:20, after the verdict exchange, and then giving feedback, instead of six separate rounds of questions.

- **X's post-test** keeps more structure (counterfactual close, "but not the only reason") than five minutes of passive reading at 10pm would usually leave. It looks like the simulator gave him the model's shape for free.
- **The evidence criterion is free.** The scenario gives Tom the post-test facts, so every session gets full marks on criterion 2 and the differences are compressed.
- **The post-test topic suits Z.** The 1928 versus 1932 vote contrast works even better for the Depression question than for Versailles, which helps the coaching session's transfer.
- **The same simulator writes and marks each post-test.** Its marks bunch together (3, 3, 3.5), and it under-credits Z's explanation and over-penalises X's link relative to Z. Its marking isn't consistent across sessions.
- **The exact timings are suspiciously convenient.** Y's paragraph lands at about 160 words without trimming, and Z's 53 minutes fits under the one-hour mark mentioned in the outcome.
