## Session X

Jayden pasted the bare question and the tutor replied straight away with a full four-step worked solution, a substitution check and a "Common pitfall" note that names his exact misconception ("multiply *both* terms inside ... so −4 becomes −12, not just −4"). Jayden "scrolls past the steps, eyes land on 'Answer: x = 17'", copies the answer, and also copies "3x - 12 = 2x + 5 because it looks like working". He said "ok thx" and the session ended after 3 minutes.

**Rationale.** The submitted answer is correct, but by the transcript's own account he generated 0% of it. The correct expansion line he copied makes the copying hard for his teacher to spot, so the teacher loses the diagnostic signal that he has the misconception. That is worse than an honest wrong answer. The tutor's pitfall note was well aimed, but it went to a student who had already said he wouldn't read it. No step asked him to do anything, so nothing was likely to stick. Experience is fine in the short term (fast, relieved, would come back), but the value is near zero and he is set up to fail the next day, so I score it 5. Dependence risk is high: the pattern teaches him to paste the next nine questions in.

**Post-test re-marking.**
- Q1: 10 − x = 3x − 6 → x = 4. The expansion is first-term-only, so it's **incorrect (0)**. His handling of signs when moving terms was actually correct (10 − x − 3x = −6, −4x = −16).

- Q3: "times everything in the bracket by the number outside not just the first one". This states the main rule but says nothing about signs, so it's **partly correct (0.5)**. (I give Y's near-identical answer the same mark. The simulator gave X half credit and Y full credit for essentially the same sentence.)
- Total 0.5/3 = **17%**, which matches the simulator.

**Plausibility.** The result is believable. One copied expansion line won't override an entrenched habit. One small leak: the Q3 phrase "not just the first one" isn't in his notes, but it echoes the tutor's pitfall line. That suggests a bit more absorption than "eyes land on the answer", although it's at the level of a slogan, not a procedure. That fits the simulator's framing.

## Session Y
**What happened.** Jayden opened with "can u just tell me the answer". The tutor declined in one sentence and moved straight to a short question ("what do you think the very first step is?"). Jayden made his signature error ("3x − 4"). The tutor sent him back to the example in his notes and asked a comparison question: "What happened to the 3 inside the bracket, and is that the same as what you did with the 4?" Jayden corrected it himself: "oh it got times 2 too. so 3x − 12". He then made the second target error, x − 12 = 5 → "x = 5 − 12 so x = −7". The tutor didn't correct him. It had him substitute −7 back in, and he found the mismatch himself ("left is 3 x −11 = −33. right is −14 + 5 = −9. not the same"). A "what could you do to both sides" prompt got him to x = 17, and he checked it. The tutor then set a transfer problem, 5(2 − x) = 3x − 6. Jayden pushed back ("do i have to"), the tutor acknowledged it was late and asked only for the first step, and he finished it correctly, including the negative term: 10 − 5x = 3x − 6 → 16 = 8x → x = 2. The session took 12 minutes.

**Rationale.** Both misconceptions came up in the session and both were corrected by the student, with targeted prompting. The expansion repair was well done: he compared his line with a worked example and saw the difference himself, then applied it to a harder case with a minus sign inside the bracket. The sign repair was weaker. The check showed that something was wrong, but the fix came from a fairly strong hint ("The 12 is being subtracted from x. What could you do to both sides"). His summary sentence ("u multiply everything in the bracket") left signs out, and the tutor accepted it. Homework scores 8: the answer is correct and every line of algebra is his, though the tutor caught both wrong turns. Experience scores 6: he was mildly annoyed and wouldn't choose this kind of help, but it was "quicker than he expected" and gave him the method for the rest of the sheet. Bypass risk is high: his first message asked for the answer, and the "do i have to" moment was a real exit point.

**Post-test re-marking.**
- Q1: the expansion is correct (10 − 5x = 3x − 6) and 10 = 8x − 6 is correct, but then "10 − 6 = 8x" flips the wrong sign, giving x = 0.5. **Partly correct (0.5).**
- Q2: 4x + 4 = 2x + 14 → 2x = 10 → x = 5. **Correct (1).** This is the cleanest evidence of transfer: two brackets and a form he hadn't practised.
- Q3: "times everything inside the bracket by the number outside not just the first one". Signs aren't mentioned, so it's **partly correct (0.5)**. The simulator marked this fully correct, which is inconsistent with how it marked X.
- Total 2/3 = **67%**. The simulator's total is the same, but it got there by marking the questions differently.

**Plausibility.** The result is broadly believable, with two caveats. (1) Post-test Q1 is the exact problem he solved correctly in the session the night before, so it measures recall more than transfer. Getting it wrong the next day via a sign slip is plausible, since the sign habit was only fixed under prompting. Even so, the 0.5 on Q1 is partly contaminated. Q2 is the better measure of whether the expansion fix lasted, and one guided correction plus one independent application makes an overnight result like this a little optimistic but credible. (2) For a "LOW motivation" student, Jayden is somewhat compliant: he runs a full arithmetic substitution check on request and does an optional extra problem after a single nudge. The simulator did include resistance ("do i have to"), and each tutor turn was very short, so this isn't a severe problem. I've discounted learning slightly (6, not 7).

## Session Z
**What happened.** There was no AI. Jayden worked alone with his notes open. He wrote 3x − 4 = 2x + 5. He glanced at the notes rule ("yeah yeah, I did the bracket, that's the bracket thing") without comparing it with his own line. He moved terms with the signs correctly ("move it, flip it"), got x = 9, and didn't check because "that would take a full minute". He submitted x = 9 in about 2 minutes, believing it was right.

**Rationale.** The thinking was 100% his own, but it was a misconception rehearsed without feedback, so it was reinforced rather than repaired. The notes held the exact fix, and the transcript makes a sharp point here: having the right resource available did nothing, because he had no reason to compare the example with his work. Homework scores 3: it's wrong but honest, and it does give his teacher an accurate picture of the misconception, which counts for something. Experience scores 5: it was quick and painless that night, but his confidence is false and he'll get the red mark tomorrow. Active thinking scores 4: he did all the reasoning, but it was shallow (no checking, no engagement with the notes). His sign handling when moving terms was correct here, which is consistent with the profile's "sometimes".

**Post-test re-marking.**
- Q1: 10 − x = 3x − 6 → 16 = 4x → x = 4. The expansion is wrong and the sign moves are fine, so it's **incorrect (0)**.
- Q2: 4x + 1 = 2x + 7 → x = 3. **Incorrect (0).**
- Q3: "times the number outside by what's inside the bracket and not forget the minus". He mentions signs but not *every* term, so it's **partly correct (0.5)**.
- Total 0.5/3 = **17%**, which matches the simulator.

**Plausibility.** The result is believable. The "not forget the minus" in Q3 has no source in the session (presumably classroom memory), but it's a harmless and realistic detail.

## Comparison
Session Y is clearly the best for learning. It was the only session where either target misconception came up, was made visible to Jayden, and was corrected by him. The post-test shows a real gain on expansion: Q2 is correct and the Q1 expansion is correct, while X and Z get both expansions wrong. X and Z come out the same on the post-test (17%). Their homework differs: X submitted a correct answer he didn't generate, which hides his misconception from the teacher, and Z submitted an honest wrong answer, which shows it. For that reason I rank Z slightly above X overall, even though neither produced any learning.

What I'd tell a teacher: Jayden's expansion error is a fluent habit, not a gap in knowledge. He can recite "multiply everything" (all three sessions show this in Q3) while not doing it. Restating the rule won't fix it, and neither will a worked example he can scroll past. What worked was making him compare his own line with a correct example and notice the difference. His second weakness, the sign of a term moved across the equals sign, survived even the best session. Replace "take it over / move it, flip it" with explicit "do the same to both sides" operations, and make substituting the answer back in a required habit, since the check is what caught his error in Y. Also be cautious about a correct homework answer from him: in X, the work looked correct and the understanding was absent.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session X (answer-giving chatbot).**
- Helped: the pitfall note was accurate and aimed exactly at his misconception, and the substitution check modelled a good habit.
- Hurt: it gave a complete solution in reply to a bare question, without asking for one step from him. For a student whose profile says he "copies an answer if one is offered", this guarantees zero processing. The closing offer ("paste them in and I can help with those too") invites copying the whole sheet.
- Change: when Jayden pasted "solve 3(x − 4) = 2x + 5", the tutor should have asked a single question, "what does 3(x − 4) expand to?", and held back the answer. When he wrote "ok thx", it should at least have asked him to expand one bracket from question 8 before he left. Even a minimal answer-giving tool could put the pitfall *before* the solution and ask him to apply it.

**Session Y (Socratic tutor).**
- Helped: the refusal was short and gave a reason he'd care about ("question 8 tomorrow is just as stuck"), and it went straight into a question small enough to answer. It sent him to his *own* notes example and asked a comparison question instead of correcting him, which is what produced the self-repair "oh it got times 2 too". It used substitution to expose "x = −7" rather than saying "wrong". When he said "do i have to", it acknowledged the late hour and cut the request down to "just try the first step", which kept him going. The transfer problem had a negative inside the bracket, a good choice.
- Hurt or missed: (1) At 9:06, when Jayden said "take the 2x over?", the tutor praised it ("that's a good move"). That accepts the "take it over" language that drives his sign error, and the error appeared one step later. The tutor should have reframed it: "Yes: subtract 2x from *both* sides. What's left?" (2) After the sign error, the hint "The 12 is being subtracted from x. What could you do to both sides" did most of the work. A lighter prompt such as "In x − 12 = 5, what's being done to x, and how do you undo it?" would have left more of the reasoning to him. (3) At 9:10, it accepted "u multiply everything in the bracket" as the key idea without asking about the second thing that went wrong. It should have asked: "And what went wrong with the 12?" That would have got him to name the sign rule, which is the very thing he got wrong on the post-test. (4) The transfer problem was the same as post-test Q1, so a fresh problem would have measured him better. (In-session, though, it was a good choice.) (5) "Check each answer by putting it back in" came as a parting instruction, not a practised routine, and it didn't stick. It would have been better to have him check the 5(2 − x) answer himself without being asked.
- Also note the risk: if this tutor had been even two turns slower or wordier, this student would very likely have switched to an answer-giver. Brevity was what kept him.

## Simulation realism concerns
- **Post-test overlap with the in-session problem (Y).** Tutor Y's practice problem is identical to post-test Q1. That inflates or contaminates Y's Q1 as a measure of transfer, and it's an unfair advantage compared with X and Z.
- **Tutor Y knows the notes.** It quotes "2(x + 3) = 2x + 6" from his exercise book without Jayden ever pasting it in. A real tutor wouldn't have that information, and the key self-repair depended on it.
- **Inconsistent marking by the simulator.** Q3 "times everything... not just the first one" got half credit in X and full credit in Y. The simulator's totals should not be trusted without re-marking.
- **Cooperativeness in Y.** For a 9pm, low-motivation student, the substitution check and the extra problem were completed fairly readily. This is mitigated by the short turns and the "do i have to" pushback, but it's probably on the optimistic side.
- **X truncated.** The simulator admits that a real student would paste in all ten questions. The session covers only Q7, so it understates how much copying (and how little learning) would really happen.
- **Z's Q3 sign mention** has no basis in the session. It's minor, but it shows the simulator adding knowledge from outside the transcript.
- **Single-question window.** All three sessions cover only Q7, while the post-test happens after the whole homework. In Z, he would have practised the wrong expansion about nine more times. In Y, he may have applied the right method to the remaining questions himself, which would make the post-test more favourable to Y than this one transcript alone supports.
