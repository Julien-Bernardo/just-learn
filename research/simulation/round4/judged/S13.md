## Session X

Her "is that right?" anxiety was handled well at [8] ("you checked it yourself by counting, so you didn't actually need me to tell you"), but the tutor still answered "Yes" every time. Each time she was guided to reject it herself, and she showed the rule working on a new case (the friend's MgO₂). She did the counting and the checking, and she left with a self-checking habit ("I counted both sides"). That is the right antidote to her "is that right?" anxiety.
- **Z** gave Maya the correct rule and a good explanation, which is much better than Y, where the misconception was reinforced. But she did none of the work. Her raw test score of 100% matches X's, and that equality is the main thing to discount. X's student showed she could do it; Z's student was shown how. The rule she can recite in Z has never been tested against her own impulse to change subscripts.
- **Y** shows what the misconception does when nothing intervenes. Her one-line notes were not enough to stop it, even though she had a real doubt and briefly tried the right move. The homework is wrong and the error is now rehearsed.

**X vs Z, the two AI sessions.** Only X tried to teach, so the direct comparison is between a teaching tutor and an answering tutor. These behaviours made the difference in X:
1. It asked for her first thought before revealing anything, which brought the misconception out.
2. It had her check H₂O₂ against her own notes' rule. She found it "balanced" and was then told the extra constraint, so the correction carried weight.
3. It tied each later relapse (H₄) back to the peroxide lesson.
4. It required an explanation in her own words, an unaided problem, and an error-diagnosis question.

Z handled the misconception only because Maya happened to ask. If she hadn't asked at [3], the subscript idea would never have been discussed directly. On engagement, X kept a medium-motivation, anxious student on task for 14 minutes. It did this with very small steps, framing her wrong answer as a good observation, and short turns. She came close to leaving only briefly, at the peroxide moment. Z kept her "engaged" only in the sense that she had nothing to leave for.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session X tutor**
- *Helped:* "What's your first thought? Even a guess" at [0] got the misconception into the open. "Count the atoms... in your new equation" at [2] let her see that H₂O₂ satisfies the class-notes rule, which shows the notes rule is necessary but not sufficient. "Think about the peroxide lesson" at [7] made her generalise the rule herself instead of having it repeated to her. The explain-your-first-idea prompt at [10] and the friend's-error question at [14] are strong retrieval and transfer moves. At [8] it reframed "is that right?" ("you checked it yourself").
- *Hurt or missed:*
  - At [5], when she asked "like H₂2O?", the tutor gave the answer: "the number goes at the very front... So 2H₂O means...". A better move was to ask: "Where could a number go so it multiplies the whole molecule rather than changing it? What would that count be?" She could also have been asked to compare H₂2O with the meaning of 2H₂O.
  - At [3] it told her H₂O₂ is peroxide. That is probably unavoidable, since she couldn't know it. But it could first have asked "Is H₂O₂ the same stuff as water? What's water's formula?" to use her own doubt, which she does voice in session Y.
  - It answered "Yes, it's right" at [8] and [13] every time. To work on her anxiety more directly, it should have asked her to say how she knows before confirming, for example "You tell me — what does your count show?".
  - The practice item (Mg + O₂) is identical to test Q1 and structurally identical to the water problem. A second item that needs a coefficient other than 2, such as N₂ + H₂ → NH₃, would have tested transfer better.

**Session Z tutor**
- *Helped:* The worked solution was accurate and well laid out, with a count table and a check step. The explanation at [4] (subscripts = identity, coefficients = how many) was clear and memorable. It offered practice twice.
- *Hurt:*
  - It gave the full answer in the first reply to a bare "balance this", without asking anything. It should have asked for her first attempt or first count.
  - "Nice work!" at [2] praised work she didn't do. That teaches her that copying gets validated.
  - At [3], when she said "I thought you could change the little numbers", it delivered a lecture. It should have said something like "Try it: if the product is H₂O₂, what is the name of that substance? Is that what the question asked for?", and then had her balance the equation with coefficients herself.
  - At [6], "Exactly right" closed the conversation. It should have made the offer concrete and low-stakes instead of open-ended, for example "Before you write it down, try one: Mg + O₂ → MgO, just tell me the first number you'd change". Practice offers phrased as "let me know if you'd like" are easy for a medium-motivation student to decline.
