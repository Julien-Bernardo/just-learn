## Comparison
**Ranking: Z > Y > X.**

All three posttests are 100% as written, so the test cannot separate the sessions (ceiling effect). The ranking rests on how robust the learning is likely to be. Z is the only session where Ravi's misconception was put under pressure: his method produced a wrong answer that he could verify, and he worked out the fix. That gives him a self-check ("does it match if I expand?") and a causal reason, both of which survive exam stress. Y gets real credit for self-correction from the notes. It also beats X on genuine ownership of the homework, but Ravi left uncertain and did not consolidate. X gave him a correct, well-phrased rule and an answer to copy. That is fast and pleasant, but it is the weakest route to dislodging a habit, because he never had to notice his own error.

**X vs Z (the two AI tutors).** These represent two philosophies more than two teaching tutors, since X answered immediately. The behaviours that made the difference in Z:
1. It asked for an attempt first, which drew out the misconception.
2. It used a cheap falsification test (expand-and-compare) instead of a correction.
3. When Ravi asked again for the trick, it gave one sentence of reason ("what makes the trick stick tomorrow when I'm not there") plus "two lines", and held firm without lecturing.
4. It asked "why did your original fail?"
5. It gave one transfer item with the layers reversed, then let him go when he said "last one then i gotta go".

The plan upfront ("a few quick steps... then one alone") respected his time pressure and made the work feel bounded. That framing is what kept a rushed, "just give me the trick" student engaged. X carries no bypass risk only because it already is the answer bot.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session X (direct-answer tutor).**
- Helped: a clear, quotable one-line rule. It named the student's exact error pattern. Its examples covered the post-test families. It confirmed his sin(5x) attempt.
- Hurt: it gave the full homework solution in the first reply, before any attempt. It worked (2x+1)⁵ for him at [2], wasting the best chance to let him catch his own habit. It accepted "nah im good" without one quick check.
- Concrete changes:
  - At [0], ask "what do you get?" first. Ravi would have written cos(3x²), and the tutor could then show the gap.
  - At [2], instead of listing worked examples, say: "Quick: d/dx (2x+1)⁵?" Then let him either get 10(2x+1)⁴ or reveal 5(2x+1)⁴.
  - At [4], when he said "nah im good", offer one 20-second item that targets his habit, such as "(4x−1)³, just the answer", before confirming the homework.

**Session Z (teaching tutor).**
- Helped: the bounded plan, attempt-first, and the falsification check rather than correction. It held firm on the expansion with a single sentence of rationale. The question "Where could a 2 come from?" was the right size of hint. It asked for self-explanation of the original error and gave a transfer item with reversed layering. It ended promptly when he signalled time.
- Hurt or could improve:
  - After [5] it never handed Ravi a crisp one-line "trick" to write down. He asked for one, and a compact takeaway in his own words ("outside' × inside'") would aid recall tomorrow. At [8], ask him to write the rule in one line himself.
  - The expansion check doesn't work for trig or exponential composites. Note briefly that the check is for building trust in the rule, not something to use on every exam item.
  - He was never asked to revisit his known habits, sin(3x) and (2x+1)⁵. A 10-second "fix your old sin(3x) answer" at [8] would have aimed squarely at the profile misconception.
  - The cos(x⁴) suggestion was left for unsupervised time, where a rushed student is unlikely to do it. Better to fold it into the session or drop it.

In X, Ravi only ever repeated the tutor. In Y, the record says he was "not 100% sure" and had not rechecked his old habits. A real rushed student with an entrenched habit would plausibly slip on at least one item in X and Y. The test simulator seems to treat "saw the rule once" as equal to "has the rule", which hides real differences between conditions.
- **Y's metacognition is a bit generous.** Getting from "feels too short" plus a heading cue to consulting the notes, then spontaneously generalising to sin(3x), is plausible for a high-motivation student, but it is on the favourable end. A rushed student who wants "the trick" might well have submitted cos(3x²).
- **Z's cooperation.** One "ugh" and then full compliance is at the cooperative end for a rushed student who wants just the trick. It is still consistent with "high motivation", and the tutor gave a good reason, so this is a minor concern.
- **Typing style.** The concise typing style matches the profile in X and Z.
