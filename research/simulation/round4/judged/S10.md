## Session X
**What happened.** This was a Socratic AI tutor. It started by asking Ava to guess. Her guess showed misconception (a) straight away: "area is 10 and perimeter is 20" and "area is 6+4" [1–2]. The tutor said her perimeter was right and gave a short definition of area. Then it asked her to draw a 6×4 grid of 1 cm squares, using her love of drawing. She replied "ok" [3], which is exactly her profile's sign of confusion. The tutor caught it ("'Ok' can mean lots of things…") and broke the task down to "draw one row… how many squares?" [4]. That got her moving. She counted 24 row by row [7] and chose 24 over 10 "because i counted the squares" [8]. She found 6×4 herself as the shortcut [9]. She also explained her own error: "i added the sides. but area is the squares inside so u have to times them" [10].

For misconception (b), the tutor gave a 5×5 square with the same perimeter. It asked her to predict before calculating. She said "same i think. because the perimeter is the same" [12], which brought the misconception into the open. She then disproved it herself ("5x5 = 25. oh its not the same") [13]. Next came an independent 8×3 transfer problem. The tutor chose it so the area would match (24) while the perimeter differs (22). Ava solved it correctly with units and generalised: "same area but different perimeter. so its the other way round too" [17].

**Test marking.**
- Q1: area 15 cm², perimeter 16 cm. Correct (1/1).
- Q2: both perimeters 16 cm, both areas correct (12 and 16), and the right conclusion with a reason. Correct (1/1).
- Q3: perimeter is all the way round, and area is "the squares inside", with cm². Correct (1/1).
- Total 100%.

**Plausibility.** This is mostly believable. Q2 almost exactly repeats the predict-then-check sequence she worked through at [11–14]. Q1 repeats the method she used alone on 8×3. A perfect score for a medium-motivation 10-year-old the next day is at the optimistic end, though. One session usually leaves some chance that adding for area creeps back under test conditions. I have set learning at 8 rather than 9–10. Her spontaneous "its the other way round too" [17] is also a bit sophisticated for her profile. The "ok" moment was realistic and well handled.

**Scores.** Both misconceptions were brought into the open, tested against evidence Ava produced herself, and repaired in her own words. That is strong repair, so misconception gets 8. Active thinking gets 7: the tutor supplied the grid idea, the definition and the unit, and Ava did the counting, comparing, predicting and checking. The homework is correct and genuinely hers, so it gets 9. Experience gets 8: there were no reprimands and it was short and concrete. However, 17 minutes on a single homework question is long for her, and the record says she would still prefer just getting the answer when in a hurry, so bypass risk is medium.

## Comparison
**Ranking: X >> Y > Z.**

X is the only session that changed Ava's thinking rather than just her answers. Both misconceptions were made explicit, tested against evidence she produced herself (her drawn grid, then her own 5×5 calculation), and replaced with an explanation in her own words. The next-day test shows transfer to a new item (Q2) that exactly targets misconception (b).

Y and Z both leave misconception (b) fully in place, and both show it on Q2. Y edges Z only because the "times for area" rule seems to have stuck, giving Y a correct Q1 and a correct homework. That rule is borrowed, and the homework is copied. Z at least produced honest work that exposes the error to her teacher, while Y's correct copied homework hides Ava's confusion from the teacher. On learning alone, Y is slightly ahead; on misconception repair they are both near zero.

Only X used a tutor that tried to teach. The behaviours that made the difference:
1. **It asked for a guess and the reasoning behind it first.** This brought "area is 6+4" into the open at once, instead of overwriting it.
2. **It caught her "ok".** It named it and broke the task into one tiny concrete step ("how many squares in the top row?"). This was the critical engagement move for this student. Y ignored the same signal.
3. **It used her strength (drawing) to generate evidence**, so the correction came from her own count, not from the tutor's authority.
4. **It asked for a prediction before calculating** on the 5×5 square, which forced misconception (b) into the open where it could be disproved.
5. **It chose a well-designed transfer item** (8×3: same area, different perimeter), which led her to generalise on her own.

On engagement, Y was "easier" in the moment, but Ava left Y still unsure and hiding it. X kept her engaged through short, concrete turns and by never treating the wrong first answer as a failure.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**X (Socratic tutor)**
- *Helped:* The guess-first opening [0]. Separating "you got the perimeter right" from the area error [2], which preserved her confidence. Catching "ok" with a smaller concrete step [4]. "Which one do you trust, and why?" [7]. Asking her to explain her original error in her own words [9]. Predict-then-check for misconception (b) [11]. A transfer item designed to produce a second insight [14–16].
- *Hurt / weak:* It stumbled over "6 rows... well, 4 rows of 6" [9], a small moment of confusion it could have avoided by asking her to say what the 6 and the 4 mean in her grid. The tutor supplied the cm² unit and the definition of area. Pointing back to her class notes ("what do your notes say area is measured in?") would have let her retrieve them herself. When she said "24 because i counted… 10 was wrong" [8], the tutor could have asked what the 10 actually measures (half the perimeter, a length), which would make the area/perimeter contrast sharper. At 17 minutes the session was long for a medium-motivation student. The 5×5 step could have been folded into the 8×3 transfer item to save time.
- *Concrete changes:* After [10], ask her to draw the 5×5 square too, rather than only calculating, so the visual "same fence, more grass" lands. End by asking her to write one sentence in her homework book explaining why area is not 6+4, as a memory aid for tomorrow.

**Y (answer-giving AI)**
- *Helped:* The explanation was accurate and included the unit-square meaning and the fence/grass analogy. It did offer a picture and a practice problem.
- *Hurt:* It gave the full solution before asking anything, so it never found out what Ava thought. When she asked "which one is the area" [3], which showed she hadn't followed the explanation, it said "Yes, you've got both numbers right!" [4]. That was false praise for numbers she had only copied. It accepted "no its ok thanks" [5] without pushing back, and it never touched misconception (b).
- *Concrete changes:* At [0], ask "what do you think the area and perimeter are, and how would you work them out?" before showing anything. When she asked "which one is the area", the tutor should have asked her to draw the rectangle and count the squares inside rather than relabelling the numbers. Before she leaves, it should set one quick check such as "a 5×5 square has the same perimeter as your 6×4 rectangle. Same area?", which takes 30 seconds and exposes misconception (b).
