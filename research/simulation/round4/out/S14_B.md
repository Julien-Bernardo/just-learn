[0] STUDENT: You paid £40 (non-refundable) for a concert ticket. On the night you feel ill and would rather stay home. A friend offers you £25 for the ticket. Using economic reasoning, should the £40 affect your decision? What should you do?

[0] TUTOR: **Short answer:** No, the £40 should not affect your decision. If you'd rather stay home, you should sell the ticket for £25.

**Why**

1. **The £40 is a sunk cost.** It's non-refundable, so it's already gone whether you go to the concert, sell the ticket, or let it go unused. Since it can't be changed by anything you do now, it shouldn't influence the decision.

2. **Only compare future costs and benefits (marginal reasoning).** You have two options:
   - **Go to the concert:** you get the enjoyment of attending while feeling ill, and you give up the £25 and the comfort of staying home.
   - **Sell the ticket:** you get £25 and you stay home, which you'd rather do.

3. **Compare the options.** You've said you'd rather stay home, so the "stay home" outcome is already worth more to you than attending while ill. Selling adds £25 on top of that. So selling is clearly better.

4. **The sunk cost fallacy.** Thinking "I paid £40, so I should go or it's wasted" is the sunk cost fallacy. The £40 is lost either way. Going would just mean you lose the £40 *and* have a bad evening *and* miss out on the £25.

**What you should do:** Sell the ticket for £25.

Also note that opportunity cost is relevant here: the opportunity cost of going to the concert is the £25 you give up (plus the comfort of staying home).
