## Comparison
**Ranking: Z > X > Y.**

All three test papers score 100%, so the ranking rests on what the sessions plausibly support. **Y** gave a complete, mostly accurate explanation, but Tariq's only cognitive act was asking "why". He declined practice and left with a rule he had never used himself. Its 100% is the least credible of the three.

**X** and **Z** both tried to teach instead of answer, and they were close. Both used substitution to let Tariq discover he was wrong, both used a 3 × 4 = 12 counterexample, both used his notes, and both set an independent practice problem. The behaviours that made Z better:
- **Z elicited the misconception; X asserted it.** Z asked "What was your thinking there?" at line 3 and got Tariq to put his faulty rule into words [5]. X told him "I think it's line 3". Z's repair was aimed at a belief the student had stated. X's was aimed at the tutor's hypothesis.
- **Z had the student choose the check.** "How could you test whether…" led to "sub them back in?". X simply told him to substitute. Z's version makes the habit more likely to be his.
- **Z's practice problem reproduced the original trap** (x² − 7x = −10), so it directly tested the repaired idea. X's practice (x² + 2x = 15) also needed rearranging, and it usefully produced a sign error that Tariq self-corrected. That is a genuine plus for X: it is the only session where the student made and fixed a new error on his own.
- **Z avoided two small missteps in X:** the invented "You mentioned class notes", and calling the valid step of moving the 6 a "slip".

For keeping this student engaged, both worked: neither student left, and both sessions ran under 15 minutes. Z's opening was better suited to a "tell me if I'm right" student. Within four short exchanges, Tariq had established for himself that he was wrong ("ok both wrong"), so his main question was answered fast. X got to "it's wrong" just as quickly (at [3]), but then lost momentum at [4] ("so how do i do it then") by detouring to "paste your notes". Y obviously had the lowest bypass pressure, but only because it was already the answer bot.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session X**
- *Helped:* It asked explicitly about the faint sign before doing anything [0], which is the correct handling of an ambiguous photo. Substitution let the student discover the error himself. The 3 × 4 = 12 counterexample was quick and concrete. It asked for a one-sentence "why" [8]. It withheld the verdict on the practice answer and asked "check x = 5" instead [13], which produced genuine self-correction. The final x(x − 5) = 0 item neatly linked back to his original line 2.
- *Hurt:* "You mentioned class notes" [4] refers to something the student never said. In a real interaction this looks like the tutor knows things it shouldn't, or it confuses the student. The tutor should have asked "Do you have notes on the steps?". "I think it's line 3" gave away the diagnosis: when the student found 72 ≠ 0, the tutor should have asked "Which line do you think it goes wrong on, and what were you thinking there?". Calling the move of the 6 "the first slip" mislabels a valid step. It should have been framed as "valid, but it took you away from = 0, which is the form the method needs".
- *Shared material:* Only one problem was visible and it was fully addressed. The tutor used the student's own working well, tying the error to line 1 and line 3. It handled the blurry sign well by asking instead of assuming. No link was involved.

**Session Y**
- *Helped:* It answered "is this right?" clearly and immediately. It noted its reading of the faint bracket ("I'm reading the bracket as x − 5, which matches the question"), which is reasonable handling of the ambiguity. The "zero is special" explanation in [4] was clear and well chosen. It offered practice twice.
- *Hurt:* It solved the entire problem in the first message, leaving the student nothing to do. That is the main failure. The x − 5 = 1 sentence in [4] is muddled and could confuse him. It accepted "ill try it later" and confirmed "x = 2 or x = 3 right" without asking him to produce anything. When Tariq asked "why cant u do it my way", the tutor should have turned the question back to him: "Try it: 2 × 3 = 6. Is either number 6? So what's special about 0?". The first reply should have stopped after "−6 and −1 aren't right; substitute −6 and see what you get", leaving the rest for him to do.
- *Shared material:* It solved everything visible, but for the student instead of with him. It identified the error line from the photo correctly. It acknowledged the ambiguous sign, but resolved it by inference and did not ask. No link was involved.

**Session Z**

It asked "What was your thinking there?" at line 3, which surfaced the exact misconception in his words. The a × b = 12 test used his own rule against him, then contrasted it with 0.
