[1] TUTOR: Happy to help! You've got the overall shape right (rise, peak at 37°C, then a fall to zero), but a 6-mark answer needs the *mechanism* behind each stage, and a couple of phrases would lose marks.

**What to change**

1. **"The enzyme works harder because it likes the warmth"**: examiners want the particle explanation. As temperature rises, enzyme and substrate molecules have more kinetic energy, so they move faster and collide more often. More collisions with enough energy means more enzyme-substrate complexes form per second, so the rate increases.
2. **"The enzyme starts to die" / "killed"**: enzymes are proteins, not living things, so they can't die. This wording would lose marks. The correct term is **denatured**, and you need to say what happens: the heat makes the enzyme vibrate more, which breaks the bonds (e.g. hydrogen bonds) holding its tertiary structure in shape. The **active site changes shape**, so the substrate is no longer complementary and can't fit. Fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form, so the rate falls. This is permanent.
3. **"37 is the optimum"**: good, but say it's where the rate is highest, i.e. the most enzyme-substrate complexes form.

**Improved answer**

> As temperature increases from 10°C, the enzyme and substrate molecules gain more kinetic energy and move faster. This means there are more frequent collisions between the enzyme and substrate, so more enzyme-substrate complexes form and the rate of reaction increases.
>
> At 37°C, the optimum temperature, the rate is at its maximum because the frequency of successful collisions is highest.
>
> Above 37°C, the enzyme molecules vibrate more and the bonds (e.g. hydrogen bonds) holding the tertiary structure break. This changes the shape of the active site, so the substrate is no longer complementary and can no longer bind. The enzyme is denatured, which is irreversible, so fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form and the rate falls sharply, reaching zero by 60°C when all the enzymes are denatured.
