**1. A cyclist moving at 4 m/s accelerates uniformly at 2 m/s² for 5 s. How far does she travel in this time?**

This looks like the one from last night. First I need to check whether the speed is the same the whole time. It isn't, because she is accelerating. So I can't just do final speed × time.

v = u + at = 4 + 2 × 5 = 14 m/s (this is the speed at the end, not the whole time)

Average speed = (4 + 14) ÷ 2 = 9 m/s

s = 9 × 5 = 45 m

Check with s = ut + ½at²: s = 4 × 5 + ½ × 2 × 25 = 20 + 25 = 45 m. They match.

**Answer: 45 m**

**2. A train slows uniformly from 20 m/s to rest in 10 s. How far does it travel while slowing?**

The speed isn't constant here either, it goes from 20 down to 0. So I can't use 20 × 10.

u = 20, v = 0, t = 10. The average speed is (20 + 0) ÷ 2 = 10 m/s.

s = 10 × 10 = 100 m

I'll check with s = ½(u + v)t = ½ × (20 + 0) × 10 = 100 m. That's the same thing really, but it matches.

I also tried s = ut + ½at². I need a first, and a = (0 − 20) ÷ 10 = −2 m/s². So s = 20 × 10 + ½ × (−2) × 100 = 200 − 100 = 100 m. Good, that also gives 100.

**Answer: 100 m**

**3. Explain why you can't use distance = speed × time with the final speed for an accelerating object.**

Distance = speed × time only works if the speed stays the same the whole time. If an object is accelerating, its speed is changing, so it isn't going at the final speed for the whole journey. It starts slower and only reaches the final speed at the very end. So if you use the final speed × time you get a distance that is too big, because you're pretending it went that fast the whole time. I did this mistake last night, with the car going 18 m/s, and I did it again on the next question. You have to use the average of the start and end speed instead (when the acceleration is constant), or use s = ut + ½at².
