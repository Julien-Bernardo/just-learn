## Comparison
**Ranking: Y > Z >> X.**

X is a pure answer service. It is fast and pleasant, and Sam leaves with working code, a weak grasp of one of the three bugs and an untouched guess-and-check habit. Its posttest is inflated.

Y and Z are both Socratic tutors that withheld code and drove learning through hand-tracing, and they were very similar. Y edges ahead for three reasons:
1. **Verification.** At [14], when Sam reported that the run had failed, Y told him to stop and trace before changing anything. That turned a "wtf" moment into his own discovery that the first element is copied into `second`. Z took an (impossible) success report at face value, so a learning opportunity was lost and an error went undetected.
2. **Self-explanation.** At [21] Y asked Sam to name his original model and every bug in his own words. The result at [24] was a four-point account plus a meta-lesson on the habit. Z's closing summary at [28] was written by the tutor, so the consolidation was done for him.
3. **Transfer practice.** Y's second_smallest task produced a real error, `n > second`, which Sam caught by tracing. That is the strongest evidence in any session that tracing replaced guessing. Z's `[2,9,9,9,4]` prediction was a good near-transfer check, but within the same function.

**Engagement.** Both kept Sam from leaving.
- Y handled the "can u just tell me" opener better by asking what he planned to try. That surfaced the habit without confrontation, and every step after a frustrated moment was small and immediately doable.
- Z's refusal at [8] leaned on a statistic and a lecture tone. It worked, but it risks feeling preachy to a deadline-pressed student. It was rescued by an easy next question.
- Z's "three quick questions" promise was overly optimistic and could have cost trust. Y's "about 10 minutes" was also an underestimate (30 actual).

  - The trace table at [5] modelled good practice.

  - It gave complete code in the first message, so Sam never had to reason.

  - When Sam said "my autograder is failing... due in 2 hrs", the tutor should have asked him to trace `[5,1,4]` (a 2-minute task) before giving any code.

## Simulation realism concerns
- **X posttest inflation:** a student who copied the code and declined to trace produces a flawless from-memory implementation, a polished trace and a complete notes-based edge-case list, without ever having engaged with the notes. It's likely overestimated by about 20-30 points.
- **Z factual error at [13]:** the student reports that `[5,1,4]` and `[7]` pass with code that would still return 5 and 7. Session Y's record at [14] shows the correct behaviour for the same code. The tutor's acceptance of this is a tutor flaw, but the false report itself is a simulator error that shortened Z's session.
- **Y test-item overlap:** Y's tutor happened to assign post-test Q1, with a duplicates-of-min, answer-first input, as in-session practice. That partly explains its 100%.
- **Z's student** flips `n > second` to `n < second` correctly on the first try with no practice, even though the same student profile made exactly that slip in Y. This is plausible but generous.
- **Cooperation levels:** In Y and Z the student is fairly cooperative given his guess-and-check profile, but both sessions show realistic friction ("ugh", "wtf", an explicit request for code), so I don't consider it a major concern.
