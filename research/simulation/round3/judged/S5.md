## Comparison
**Ranking: Y ≈ X >> Z**, with Y narrowly first for learning and X narrowly first for efficiency and experience.

Both X and Y tried to teach rather than answer, and both followed the same strong arc: gut judgement, rough draft, a diagnostic question that makes Tom see his draft is narrative, causal sentence frames, a counter-factor, a redraft, and Tom explaining what was wrong with the first draft. Both refused to write the paragraph and linked the refusal to Tom's own risk, the oral explanation.

Differences that mattered:
- **Diagnosis tool.** Y's "would this answer 'What happened to Germany after 1919?'" is a sharper, more transferable self-check than X's "could these come from a textbook summary". Tom could run Y's test on any future paragraph.
- **Depth of explanation.** Y pushed one step further at [34], from "the treaty helped Hitler get support" to "how did appeasement lead to *war*". Tom then produced the best piece of reasoning across all three sessions. X accepted a final paragraph whose Rhineland sentence is still description.
- **Worked example.** X's printing-press example on a different topic is a good scaffold for a narrative writer: he sees what analysis looks like without anything to copy. Y gave no model and relied on frames.
- **Efficiency and engagement.** X finished in 28 minutes against Y's 40 and had fewer flashpoints. For a low-motivation student at 10pm, X's pacing is more likely to keep a real student. Y's two near-exits ([7], [23]) and its unsourced "20% lower exam scores" claim are weak points. Y recovered both times by making the next step concrete and small, which is what actually kept him.
- **Transfer.** X got an independent one-line attempt on a new question in-session. Y's independent follow-up was deferred ("maybe tmrw") and probably won't happen.

Net: Y produced slightly deeper repair and more student reasoning. X was nearly as good, faster, and more likely to be used again. Z produced no learning and actively helped disguise authorship.

- Hurt or missed: at [24], when Tom's final draft included "He then broke it by putting troops in the Rhineland in 1936", the tutor should have asked "why does that sentence matter for your argument?". As it stands, a descriptive sentence survived into the submission. The frame at [4] ("The Treaty was ___ important than ___") pushed Tom into "more important than the depression", which he later reversed. **Session Y**
