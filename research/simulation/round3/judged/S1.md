## Session X
**What happened.** This was a Socratic tutor that used a drawing. The tutor started by asking for Leo's guess ("35"), then had him draw 52 as sticks and dots and take away 20 to get 32. It then asked whether the final answer would be more or less than 32. Leo found for himself that 35 > 32 and so "35 can't be right" ([3]). This was the best moment in any of the three sessions: Leo saw the contradiction himself and the tutor didn't just tell him. The tutor then asked "can you take 7 dots from 2 dots?", pointed him to the hint box, and had him do the exchange on his drawing ("4 tens and 12 dots", [6]) and work out 12 − 7 = 5. At [8] Leo still said 3 tens ("so 35 again") and got upset ("I can't do it"). This was his second wrong attempt, which is exactly where the profile says he breaks down. The tutor was reassuring ("you're one step away") and redirected him to count the sticks that weren't crossed out, which gave 25. Leo checked it against 32 himself. At [11] the tutor asked Leo to describe his first method ("took the 2 from the 7 cos 7 is bigger"), but then the tutor explained the fix rather than having Leo state it. On the transfer question (63 − 28) Leo exchanged correctly (13 − 8 = 5) but made **the same tens error again** (6 − 2 = 4, giving 45). One prompt fixed it.

**Scoring rationale.** Leo did a lot of the thinking here: about 55% of the steps, all tied to a visual model, plus the self-check against 32. That justifies active_thinking 7. The homework is correct and is his own work, done with step-by-step scaffolding (7). The session showed two weaknesses clearly: (a) Leo forgets to reduce the tens after an exchange (he did it twice, and both times the tutor fixed it), and (b) the "bigger minus smaller" belief was never put into words by Leo as a rule. The tutor said it for him. At 13 minutes the session ran past his ~10-minute attention span ("getting wriggly"), and he was upset at minute 8. Experience gets 6.

**Test marking.**
- Q1 63 − 28 = 35: **correct** (1). This was the transfer question from the session, and he says "I did it yesterday", so partly recall.
- Q2 41 − 16 = 35: **wrong** (0). He exchanged correctly (11 − 6 = 5) but did 4 − 1 = 3 without taking off the exchanged ten. This is exactly the error he made twice in the session.
- Q3 Amir: "Yes, Amir is right": **wrong** (0). He noticed "4 is smaller than 9…" but fell back on "the 9 is bigger so you do 9 take away 4".
- Score 1/3 = **33%**.

**Plausibility.** Believable, maybe slightly harsh. Q2 is very well supported by the session, because the tens-decrement error showed up twice and was only ever fixed by a tutor prompt. Q3 also fits. Leo never stated the rule himself, and he only exchanges when cued by "top ones smaller". When he has to evaluate someone else's work, nothing cues him, so it makes sense that he reverts. The self-discovery at [3] (35 > 32) might reasonably have left some trace, for example doubting Amir's 25. So 33% is at the low end of what this session supports. I'd estimate 33–50%, and learning is scored 4.

## Session Y
**What happened.** The tutor promised "about 5 minutes" and asked for Leo's guess ("35"). It then used a sweets analogy: "2 sweets, give me 7?" This brought the conflict straight to the surface at [2]: "you cant, you only have 2. but he says at school you do 7 take away 2". That was a well-targeted check on the misconception. Asked how to get more cubes, Leo said "idk", and the tutor sent him to the hint box. The tutor then **stated** the comparison itself ("in 52 the top ones number is 2 and the bottom is 7, so 2 is smaller") rather than asking Leo to compare. Leo gave "5 tens and 12 ones" (the same tens-decrement error as in X) and got upset. The tutor praised the correct part and gave one small concrete step ("you pick one stick up… how many are left?"). That fixed it, and Leo then did 12 − 7 and 4 − 2 himself to get 25. At [10] the tutor asked why his first idea didn't work. Leo's reply was muddled ("he did 7 take away 2. cos you cant take 7 from 2"), and the tutor accepted it and restated it cleanly. Leo then did 63 − 28 **alone** and got it right, with the working ("crossed out a ten, 5 tens and 13 ones…"). The session ended at about 12 minutes, kindly and promptly.

**Scoring rationale.** Leo did less of the reasoning here than in X (about 40%). The tutor supplied the comparison and the sweets frame, and the hint box supplied the exchange. Active_thinking is 6. But the independent transfer question is real evidence of learning, and it is stronger evidence than X's, where the transfer question needed a prompt. The homework is correct and is his own work (7). The sweets analogy confronted the misconception directly in Leo's own words, but his "why" answer at [10] was garbled and the tutor glossed over it. That means the repair is only partly verified (misconception 6). Experience is 7: the session was close to his attention span, was kind, and had one upset that he recovered from quickly.

**Test marking.**
- Q1 63 − 28 = 35: **correct** (1). Recall of the session's transfer question ("that's the one from yesterday").
- Q2 41 − 16 = 25: **correct** (1), with the exchange and the reduced tens (3 − 1 = 2) shown explicitly.

**Plausibility.** Optimistic. Q1 is recall. Q2 is believable, because Leo did the solo transfer correctly in the session, including the tens. Q3 is the stretch. For a 7-year-old who reads slowly to give a fluent, fully worked critique ("It's 4 on top… 2 tens and 14 ones… 15") the next day goes beyond what the session shows. ## Comparison
**Ranking: Y > X >> Z.**

Z is clearly last. It handed over the answer and the working at the exact moment Leo's misconception came up ([2]), and the test shows the misconception completely intact.

X and Y are close. Both teach rather than answer, both send Leo to the hint box, both hit an "I can't do it" moment with the same tens-decrement error, and both recover with praise plus a smaller step. On pure tutoring moves, **X is arguably stronger**. The estimate-and-check (take 20 from 52 to get 32, so the answer must be less than 32, so 35 is impossible) let Leo *discover* that his answer was wrong, and that is the most robust kind of misconception repair. The drawing also kept him actively building the model. **Y wins on the outcomes that matter for this student**:
- It was shorter and more tightly paced. It promised 5 minutes, ended at about 12, and never lost him. X ran to 13 minutes with a "wriggly" child, and the transfer question came after his attention had gone.
- The sweets analogy got Leo to say the conflict out loud in his own words ("you cant… but at school you do 7 take away 2"). In X, the tutor did the equivalent naming for him at [11].
- Y's fix for the tens error ("pick one stick up, how many are left on the table?") makes the *action* of removing a ten physical. X's fix ("count the sticks NOT crossed out") was a counting instruction that Leo had to be given again on the next question, and he made the same error again on the test (Q2). In Y, the student did the transfer question with the tens reduced correctly on his own, and that is the best evidence of learning in either transcript.

X's test result (33%) is believable. Y's (100%) is inflated, and the true gap between them is smaller than the raw scores suggest. I still rank Y ahead on the session evidence. For keeping Leo engaged and not leaving, Y was better: it was short, it set expectations, and it had one upset that was handled quickly. X's longer chain of questions ran the higher risk of the parent giving in and asking for the answer.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session X (Socratic tutor using a drawing)**
- Helped: asking for a guess first; the estimate-and-check against 32, which let Leo refute 35 himself; using the hint box rather than the tutor's own words; asking Leo to check 25 before confirming it; asking Leo to describe his original method at [11].
- Hurt: at [11], after Leo said "I took the 2 from the 7 cos 7 is bigger", the tutor explained the rule itself. It should have asked "Which number is on top in the ones? So which do we start with?" and let Leo state the rule. That is exactly the reasoning the Amir question tested, and Leo failed it. When Leo made the tens error at [8], the tutor fixed it this once without turning it into a routine. It should have added a fixed written step, such as "cross out the 5 and write 4 above it", so that the tens decrement becomes part of the procedure. The same error came back at [12] and on test Q2. Pacing was also a problem. "About 5 short steps" turned into about 12 exchanges. With a 7-year-old with a 10-minute attention span, the tutor should have cut the estimate step shorter or skipped asking for a one-line explanation on the transfer question.
- Concrete change: when Leo says "I can't do it" on the second wrong attempt, give one physical action ("pick up one stick and put it in the ones pile; now how many sticks are left?") rather than another counting question. Then make "write the new tens number above" a required step.

**Session Y (Socratic tutor using an analogy)**
- Helped: setting a short time expectation; the sweets analogy aimed straight at the misconception; sending him to the hint box; handling the upset with praise for the correct part plus one tiny physical step; finishing with an independent transfer question and ending the session before he fell apart.
- Hurt: at [5] the tutor did the key comparison for Leo ("2 is smaller"). It should have asked "Which is smaller, the top ones or the bottom ones?" At [10], Leo's explanation was garbled ("he did 7 take away 2. cos you cant take 7 from 2"), and the tutor called it "a good reason" and rewrote it. It should have asked one short follow-up such as "So which number is on top in the ones, Leo?" to confirm the rule was his own. At [4], when Leo said "idk", the tutor went straight to the hint box. One extra nudge, like "what if you broke one of the tens sticks?", might have let him come up with the exchange himself.
- Concrete change: add a quick "spot the mistake" item at the end (e.g. "Someone says 43 − 18 = 35. Right or wrong?"). That tests the actual misconception, not just the procedure, and takes 30 seconds.

**Session Z (answer bot)**
- Helped: the explanation was correct and included an adding-back check, and the tone was kind.
- Hurt: it gave the full answer before Leo had said anything. When Leo asked "why can't you do 7 take away 2", it gave a paragraph pitched at an adult instead of a question Leo could answer (e.g. "If you have 2 sweets, can you give me 7?"). When the parent asked "can you just tell me what to write", it handed over the working and then solved the next question in full as well.
- Concrete change: first ask for Leo's answer. When he says 35, turn it into one concrete check he can do himself. When he gets upset, shrink the step instead of handing over the working, and ask the parent to keep the answer hidden while Leo works.
