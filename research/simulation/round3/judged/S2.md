## Comparison
**Ranking: Y ≈ X >> Z**, with Y narrowly ahead.

X and Y are both AI tutors that tried to teach. They were strikingly similar in structure: elicit a first guess, test it on 1/2 vs 1/4, near-quit after the second wrong answer, recovery through specific praise plus a fully worked parallel example, the student finds 15 and converts, a reflection on the first idea, then a solo problem. Both kept Mia in the chat, and the same move did it each time. When she said "im bad at maths can we stop", both tutors immediately (a) named something specific she had done right and (b) stopped asking open questions and *showed* a complete parallel example before handing back a small, concrete step ("count in 3s and in 5s"). That switch from questioning to worked example at the frustration point is the single behaviour most responsible for neither student leaving.

The differences:
- **Core misconception, the "why".** X got Mia to say the reason herself ("the slices are littler when theres 4 of them"). Y's tutor said "So bigger numbers… doesn't work" for her. X is better here.
- **The notes-induced error ("change only the bottom").** Both students made it (X: 2/15; Y: 2/3 → 2/5). Y caught it with a crisp counterexample, explained the rule ("whatever you do to the bottom, you do to the top") and had her name it in the reflection. X fixed it locally with the pieces model and never came back to it. Y is better here.
- **Practice item.** X's 3/4 vs 2/3 tested the full both-fractions method, but the misconception would have given the right answer anyway. Y's 3/4 vs 5/8 directly defeats the misconception but only needs one conversion. Each covers what the other misses.
- **What triggered the second wrong answer.** X's open "can you think of a bottom number?" invited a blind guess ("8?"), which is an avoidable failure. Y's second wrong answer was a genuine misconception that needed surfacing, so the failure was productive. Y's sequencing is better.
- **Engagement cost.** X pushed past "do i have to". Y's practice item was accepted without a fight. Y's broken "about 5 minutes" promise is a small trust cost.

Taken together, Y repaired more of what actually goes wrong for this student (both misconceptions, named), and X produced slightly stronger evidence of independent method transfer. The next-day results are identical, and both are inflated by Q3. Z is far behind: copied homework and the misconception back the next day.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session X (AI tutor)**
- *Helped:* asking for a guess and a reason first. Testing her rule on 1/2 vs 1/4, which she trusts. Asking "Why is 1/4 the smaller one?" so she produced the reason. Specific, true praise at the quit point plus a worked parallel. Fixing 2/15 with the pieces model rather than just saying it was wrong. A solo problem that checked her method, with the instruction "send me your answer and one line of working".
- *Hurt:* "Can you think of a bottom number that both thirds and fifths could be changed into?" is a guessing prompt for a child who is not fluent yet. When the student had just finished [4], the tutor should have gone straight to "Count in 3s and count in 5s. Where do the lists meet?" That would likely have avoided the wrong answer that triggered the quit. After the 2/15 slip was fixed, the reflection should also have asked "what did you nearly do with the top number?", because that slip is the one her class notes set up. The solo problem should have been one where bigger numbers lose, such as 3/5 vs 2/3 reversed, or 5/8 vs 2/3, so it tests the misconception as well as the method.
- *Concrete change:* when Mia said "do i have to", offer a choice of two short problems rather than "just this one". It keeps her in control and costs nothing.

**Session Y (AI tutor)**
- *Helped:* the pizza test of her rule. Letting her bring up her notes-based method and then disproving "2/3 → 2/5" with her own trusted pair (1/2 → 1/4). The worked parallel that explicitly multiplied top and bottom. Having her name both errors in the reflection. A practice item chosen to defeat the misconception.
- *Hurt:* at [2], when Mia said "but 4 is bigger than 2 hmm", the tutor drew the conclusion for her. It should have asked "Why is 1/4 less pizza even though 4 is bigger?" so she produced the piece-size reason. "It should take about 5 minutes" set an expectation that was then broken, which is risky with a 15-minute attention span. "That is what keeps the fraction the same" was asserted, not shown. One line tying it to pieces ("each third is cut into 5, so 2 thirds become 10 fifteenths") would have given the rule meaning.
- *Concrete change:* the solo problem should have needed both fractions converted *and* had the bigger numbers on the smaller fraction (e.g. 5/8 vs 2/3). That would check both repaired errors at once.

**Session Z (general chatbot)**
- *Hurt:* it gave the answer and a ready-to-copy explanation before Mia had thought at all. When she said "but 3/5 has bigger numbers", it should have turned that into a test ("Which is more pizza, 1/2 or 1/10?") instead of explaining the result. When she asked "what do i write for the explain part", it should have asked her to say it in her own words, rather than handing over a sentence that she copied verbatim. Decimals (0.67) are beyond Year 5 and added nothing.
