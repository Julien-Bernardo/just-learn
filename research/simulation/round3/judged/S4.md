## Comparison

**Ranking: Z > Y > X.**

All three marked tests are 100%, so the ranking rests on how much the sessions actually support that score.

**X** is the weakest for learning, even though the student liked it and it was the only session that explicitly taught a general method for choosing equations. Handing over the answer at [0] took away the moment that matters most for this profile: producing 108 and finding out for herself why it is wrong. Her repair of the misconception rests on agreeing with an explanation. The one practice question was from rest and could never trigger the error. The first reply also reinforced the habit her profile flags, asking for the answer to feel safe. Her perfect test score is the least believable of the three.

**Y and Z** are both Socratic tutors. They follow almost the same path: first attempt → speed list → too big or too small? → panic and a request for the answer → "one fair speed" → cross-check with a second equation → explain in her own words → practice with u ≠ 0. The differences that matter:
- **Equation selection.** Z made it a goal from the start ("exactly the thing we'll work on") and required "which equation you chose and why" in the practice. Y never dealt with her opening problem in general. This is the main reason Z prepares her better for Q4, which tests exactly that.
- **The relapse.** Y's practice drew out a full relapse (70 m), which was then fixed after a minimal prompt. Z's student caught it before writing it down. Y's version gives better diagnostic evidence. Z's shows a more mature repair. Both are good.
- **Handling the panic.** Y's response at [7] is better for this student: it validates her, gives a concrete time cost ("about 2 more minutes"), says why it matters for the exam and gives a smaller step, all without moralising. Z's response at [10] works but includes a statistic used for persuasion. For an anxious student who already "feels bad" about asking for answers, this risks adding guilt.
- **Efficiency.** Y took 17 minutes and Z took 26. For an anxious student, Y's pace is easier to sustain and probably carries lower bypass risk across many sessions. Z's extra time bought general equation-selection practice, which is worth it six weeks before an exam.

For keeping her engaged, Y handled it slightly better: it was shorter, warmer at the crisis point and ended more confidently. For durable, exam-relevant learning, Z edges ahead because it addressed both of her difficulties: the misconception and which equation to choose.

## Tutor behaviour critique

- Helped: a clear explanation with a speed list over time at [3]; the missing-variable table at [7] is excellent; it offered practice and checked her work.
- Hurt: a full solution in the first reply, before she had shown any thinking. "Does that make sense?" invites passive agreement. The practice question was a near-copy of the homework.
- Changes: when she said "I never know which one to use", the tutor should have asked her to list u, v, a, s and t herself, then used the table to choose. When she raised 108 at [2], it should have asked "what was the car's speed at t = 2 s? Was it 18?" and let her find the problem, rather than explaining it. The practice question should have had u ≠ 0, or been a deceleration, to test whether the misconception comes back. After "I think I get it", it should have offered one more question that tempts the error, not let her close.

**Y (Socratic tutor)**
- Helped: getting her first attempt out right away exposed the misconception. Concrete speed values. The "more or less than 108?" question before any formula. The response to the panic at [7], with a time estimate and a smaller step. The check-it-yourself prompt at [14] instead of marking her wrong. Naming the relapse pattern at [16].
- Hurt: it never dealt with her actual opening question (choosing equations). At [9] it effectively told her which equation to use ("u, a, t and s ... and no v") instead of having her do the matching. It ended without a general rule she could take away or a link to the other equations on her sheet.
- Changes: at [9], ask "which variable don't you have or need? Which equation on your sheet doesn't contain it?" and let her do the matching. Before closing, give one question with no time given (like Q4), or at least point out that each equation leaves out one variable.

**Z (Socratic tutor)**
- Helped: it set expectations up front (together, then alone). It praised her correct steps specifically at [4]. The diagnostic question at [7] ("was the car ever going 18 m/s in the first 5 seconds?") let her conclude for herself that the answer was too big. Requiring "which equation and why" in the practice. Generalising to "what do I know, what do I need" for the remaining questions.
- Hurt: the statistic at [10] is unsourced and moralising, and the wrong tone for an anxious student who already feels guilty about asking for answers. The session was long (26 minutes), with slow steps between [4] and [7] when she said she couldn't see the point of the speed list. The hint at [13] still listed the variable set for her.
- Changes: when she said "Could you just tell me the answer?" at [9], drop the statistic. Say something like "You've just done the hard part. ## Simulation realism concerns

- **Practice question = test Q1.** In both Y and Z, the tutor's practice question (u = 4 m/s, a = 2 m/s², t = 5 s) is test Q1 word for word. The test simulator saw this, and Y's answer even says "This looks like the one from last night". This inflates Q1 for Y and Z, and the test cannot measure transfer of the misconception fix on Q1.
- **Ceiling effect.** All three simulated tests are perfect, including Q4 (choosing an equation with no time given), which only X directly prepared for. Y's and Z's Q4 answers also add inventive cross-checks the sessions never modelled. The next-day simulator seems biased towards full success and has little sensitivity to differences between sessions. That is most implausible for X, given how passive that session was, and for Y on Q4.
- **Negative acceleration.** Y and Z both check Q2 by calculating a = −2 m/s² and substituting it, which was never practised in any session. That is plausible for a strong student but generous for an anxious one.
- **Student behaviour.** In Y and Z, Priya's panic and request for the answer appear exactly when the profile predicts and fit it well. In X, she shows no anxiety at all. That is consistent with getting the answer at once, but her spontaneous "why is mine wrong?" at [2] makes her somewhat more self-driven than an anxious student who just received a safe answer. It is plausible given high motivation, but convenient.
- **Timing.** Z's 26 minutes for a single question, with gaps of 3-4 minutes between turns, is believable for an anxious student working carefully, but makes Z look more costly than it would be in practice.
- **Unverifiable tutor claim.** Z's "about 20% lower on exams" statistic is presented as fact without a source. A real deployment should not give unsourced numbers to students.
