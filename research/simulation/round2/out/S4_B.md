[2] STUDENT: Thank you. But I did it a different way first and I don't understand why its wrong. I used v = u + at to get v = 18 m/s and then did distance = speed x time, so 18 x 6 = 108 m. Why can't I do that?

[3] TUTOR: Good question, and it's a really common mix-up.

The equation distance = speed × time only works when the speed is **constant**. In this question the car is accelerating, so its speed keeps changing:

- At t = 0 s: 0 m/s
- At t = 2 s: 6 m/s
- At t = 4 s: 12 m/s
- At t = 6 s: 18 m/s

18 m/s is only the speed at the very **end**. For most of the 6 seconds the car is going slower than that. If you use 18 × 6 you are pretending the car was at top speed the whole time, which overestimates the distance (that's why you got 108 m, double the real answer).

Because the acceleration is uniform, the speed increases steadily, so you can use the **average** speed instead: (0 + 18)/2 = 9 m/s. Then 9 × 6 = 54 m. That is exactly what the equation s = ½(u + v)t on your sheet does.

Does that make sense?

[5] TUTOR: Exactly. 18 m/s is the speed at the *end* of the 6 seconds. It started at 0 and gradually sped up, so the typical (average) speed over the journey is lower, 9 m/s here. For constant acceleration from rest, the average speed is always half the final speed.

**Example from your question:** you know u, a, t and want s. The variable you don't have and don't need is v, so use s = ut + ½at².
