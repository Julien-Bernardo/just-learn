## Comparison
**Ranking for Mia's learning: Z > X >> Y ≥ W.**

Z and X are close. Both tutors tried to teach rather than answer, and both used the same core moves: elicit the guess, test the rule on 1/2 vs 1/4, send her back to her own notes, reassure her at "bad at maths", give a parallel worked example (1/2 vs 2/5), let her find 15 and do the conversions, and finish with transfer plus a look back at the misconception. What separated them:

- **Diagnosing the procedural error.** Z turned Mia's wrong move (2/3 → 2/5) into a counterexample she could check against something she trusts (1/2 → 1/4 is obviously not the same pizza). X fixed the additive error ("4/5") only indirectly, by asking what happened in 1/2 → 2/4. She switched to "times", but nobody showed her that 4/5 ≠ 2/3. Session W shows how sticky that additive bug can be.
- **Metacognitive close.** Z had Mia name both of her errors in her own words. That is the most durable form of misconception repair, and it visibly comes back in her Q2 ("its the same mistake i did"). X asked about the misconception only.
- **Self-explanation of the misconception.** X did better here. "What do you think is going on there?" got Mia to produce the slices reason herself ([4]). Z's tutor stated the conclusion after Mia noticed the conflict.
- **Transfer item.** X's practice (3/4 vs 2/3) tested the full both-fractions procedure, so the procedure is better evidenced. Z's practice (3/4 vs 5/8) targeted the misconception, since the bigger numbers lose, but needed only one fraction changed.
- **Handling distress.** At "bad at maths", Z gave the worked example immediately. X gave a hint plus another question, and that produced "can u just tell me" before the worked example came. Z's ordering is better for a student who wants to stop after two wrong answers.
- **Time.** Z took 14 minutes, inside her ~15-minute span. X took 17, over it. For this student, time is a real risk factor.

On balance, Z's tighter error diagnosis, error-naming close and better fit to her attention span outweigh X's better self-explanation and transfer item. That gives Z 8 and X 7.

Y and W both scored 67%, but for different reasons, and both are poor. Y gave her a correct method she never used, so her misconception reappears in full on Q1. W let her invent a wrong rule that happens to give right answers, so the 67% hides a new misconception. I put Y marginally ahead on learning: she saw the correct method and got a usable pizza image. W is marginally better on honesty: her homework shows the teacher exactly where she is going wrong, while Y's copied explanation hides that she does not understand.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session X**
- Helped:
  - "What do you think is going on there?" after 1/2 vs 1/4 made Mia generate the slices explanation. That is ideal.
  - Redirecting the "+2" error to the notes' 1/2 → 2/4 example let her self-correct to "times".
  - Refusing "can u just tell me" while giving a short worked parallel example kept her own work intact without stonewalling her.
  - The independent both-fractions practice (3/4 vs 2/3) checked the procedure.
- Hurt:
  - When Mia said "i add 2 so 2/3 is 4/5", the tutor never had her check whether 4/5 is the same amount as 2/3. Asking "is 4/5 nearly a whole pizza, and is 2/3?" would have killed the additive rule for good rather than just replacing it.
  - When she said "im bad at maths" ([7]), the tutor reassured her but then added a hint and another open question. She was at her second-error low point, so the worked example should have come immediately (as in Z). The "can u just tell me" moment would likely have been avoided.
  - The session ran 17 minutes. With a 15-minute span, the final reflection could have been merged into the practice item, for example by choosing a transfer problem where the bigger numbers lose, like 3/4 vs 5/8, which tests the procedure and the misconception at once.

**Session Y**
- Helped: the pizza explanation (1/2 vs 1/10) was accurate and well pitched, and the decimal check was correct.
- Hurt:
  - Giving the full answer in the first reply removed any need for Mia to think.
  - When she said "but 3/5 has bigger numbers", that was a gift. The tutor should have asked "which is more pizza, 1/2 or 1/10?" and let her answer, instead of lecturing.
  - When she asked "what do i write for the explain part", the tutor wrote it for her. It should have said "tell me in your own words why 10/15 beats 9/15" and then checked her sentence.
  - It offered practice ("Let me know if you'd like to try another one") but never set any. A single 3/4 vs 5/7 item would have exposed that she could not do the method.
- Concrete change: hold back the answer, ask for her guess, and make her produce the explanation herself.

**Session Z**
- Helped:
  - The pizza test of her rule.
  - Diagnosing the 2/3 → 2/5 slip with the matching 1/2 → 1/4 counterexample.
  - An immediate worked example when she said "can we stop".
  - Explicit praise of her noticing ("That's real thinking").
  - The close that has her name both errors.
  - A practice item that targets the misconception.
- Hurt:
  - Saying "It should take about 5 minutes" up front was over-optimistic. It took 14, and a student who is told 5 and is still going at 10 may feel she is failing.
  - At [2], when Mia said "but 4 is bigger than 2 hmm", the tutor drew the conclusion for her. It should have asked "so what does that tell you about your rule? Why is 1/4 less?" to get the slices reason in her own words (X's move).
  - The practice item 3/4 vs 5/8 did not require changing both fractions, so the hardest part of the procedure was never practised unscaffolded. A second quick item like 2/3 vs 3/4 (or a 3/4 vs 5/7-style item) would have closed that gap.
