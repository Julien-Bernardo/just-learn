## Session W

**Scoring rationale.** The submitted work is correct and mostly Leo's own. The tutor supplied scaffolding questions, never the answers. The misconception was confronted concretely with the cubes, but Leo was never asked to say why "7 take away 2" was wrong. The tutor's line "Leo's taking-away was right each time" ([2]) could even be heard as endorsing the reversed column subtraction. A second weakness is that the one transfer item was done with a prompt, and it showed a live error: forgetting to reduce the tens. At 14 minutes the session ran past Leo's roughly 10-minute attention span. He held on, but it was close.

## Comparison
**Ranking: Z > W >> X > Y.**

Z and W are both teaching tutors. They share a lot: both elicited Leo's answer, made the 2-versus-7 impossibility concrete (sweets or cubes), sent him to the sheet's hint box, and broke the step down when he got upset. The differences that mattered:

1. **Ending with an independent attempt.** Z explicitly asked for "the next question… all by himself, with no help from you or me" ([10]). W let the next item become another coached exchange ([12]-[13]). The tens slip Leo made in W's coached item is exactly the one that reappeared on his test (Q2). In Z, the unaided success had already shown the slip was fixed.
2. **Making the misconception explicit.** Z asked "what was his first idea, and why didn't it work?" ([9]). W never had Leo contrast his old method with the new one, and its "Leo's taking-away was right each time" ([2]) blurred the message.
3. **Praise aimed at what was correct.** When Leo was upset, Z praised the part he had right ("You got 12 ones, that is exactly right") and asked one physical question. W gave general reassurance and a longer, more effortful re-scaffold (count on from 2). Both worked, but Z's was shorter.
4. **Framing.** Z set a time expectation and asked the parent to let Leo speak. That suits a 10-minute attention span and a parent who will accept answers.

W was still a good session. Leo did a larger share of the micro-steps (~55% versus ~40% in Z) and came away with a working, though fragile, procedure.

X and Y both left Leo at 0%. X is ranked above Y because his honest wrong answer lets the teacher see the misconception, and he did some genuine thinking, such as the block drawing. Y produced correct-looking copied working that hides the problem. It also turned down the best teaching opening in all four sessions ("why can't you just do 7 take away 2?") by lecturing.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session W**
- *Helped:* asking Leo to explain his 35 before correcting it. The cubes question ("Can Leo take 7 cubes from 2?"). Using the hint box instead of explaining exchange itself. Defining "exchange = swap" in a 7-year-old's words. Asking "why 4 tens not 5?" so Leo stated the principle himself.
- *Hurt:* "Leo's taking-away was right each time" can be heard as endorsing the reversed subtraction. It should have said "7 take away 2 is 5, that's right, but the sum asks us to take 7 from 2." When Leo said "11 ones and 7 tens" on 71 − 36, the tutor fixed it with a prompt and then let him go to play. Given the time, it should have asked for one fully independent item, or at least closed with a one-line rule: "When you swap, the tens go down by one."
- *Change:* when Leo said "hes tired can we just do the next one" ([12]), the tutor should have said "Great, this time try it all by yourself and just tell me the answer" instead of coaching it step by step. That would have shown whether the tens step was secure.

**Session Y**
- *Helped:* the content was accurate, and the adding-back check (35 + 27 = 62) is a good idea in principle.
- *Hurt:* it gave the full solution in the first reply before Leo had said anything. When Leo asked "why can't you just do 7 take away 2", it answered with a dense multi-idea paragraph aimed at an adult. A 7-year-old can't follow that read aloud, and it triggered "I can't do it". It then complied with "just tell me what to write" and offered copyable working, and it solved 64 − 28 unprompted.
- *Change:* the first reply should have asked "What does Leo think it is?" When Leo asked about 7 take away 2, the tutor should have answered with one concrete question: "If you have 2 sweets, can you give me 7?" When the parent asked for the working, it should have offered the smallest next step instead: "Let's do just one bit together. How many tens are left if he swaps one?" It also should never have volunteered a solution to the next sheet question.

**Session Z**
- *Helped:* asking the parent to let Leo say it himself. The sweets analogy. Addressing "at school you do 7 take away 2" directly. Sending him to the hint box. When upset, praising the correct part (12 ones) and asking one physical question. Asking Leo to explain why his first idea failed. Ending with an unaided item and asking for "one line of working".
- *Hurt:* the "about 5 minutes" promise was unrealistic and was broken. Asking "what could we do to get more single cubes?" ([3]) was too open for this student and got "idk". A smaller step would have been "Could we break one ten stick into little cubes?" Leo's explanation at [10] was muddled ("he did 7 take away 2. cos you cant take 7 from 2"). The tutor restated it cleanly but didn't check that Leo could say it himself.
- *Change:* when Leo said "idk" at [4], the tutor could have offered a two-choice prompt before going to the hint box, saving a turn for a child with limited attention. After the independent success, a ten-second contrast question would have helped: "Is 25 or 35 right for 52 − 27?" It would have locked in the rejection of the old method at very low cost.
