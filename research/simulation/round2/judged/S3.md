Marking convention for Q3: the key says "Multiply every term inside, keeping signs." I gave full credit to any answer that clearly says every term or both terms must be multiplied, because that is the core of the rule, and I note where signs are left out. An answer that leaves out the "every term" idea gets half credit at most.

## Comparison
**Ranking: Y > W > Z > X.**

Y and W both used tutors that tried to teach rather than give the answer, and both ended at 100% on the test. Y is ahead for three reasons.

1. **How the error was shown to him.** W fixed the bracket by pointing at the notes ("which parts did you multiply by 3?") and fixed −7 by telling him it was wrong. Y had him substitute numbers and find the contradiction himself, both times. Evidence he produces is more convincing to a student than a reminder, and the test shows the difference in practice. In Y's Q1 the old sign error comes back and **he catches it himself by checking**. W's student has no such safety net. His perfect test depends on the slip simply not happening.
2. **A general tool versus a slogan.** W's summary is "multiply everything in the bracket and flip the sign when it moves". That is useful, but the tutor itself endorsed the "flip when it crosses" shortcut, which is the same shortcut behind the sign misconception. Y left him with "multiply everything" plus "check by plugging back in", and the second works on every remaining question and every future one.
3. **Reflection and the up-front deal.** Y asked him to name his own two errors ([8]–[9]). W only asked for a summary of the method. Y's opening deal ("2 minutes, then you do the next one alone") set expectations and made the solo transfer question part of the deal. W had to negotiate the practice question when he pushed back, and offered him the chance to skip it.

W's strength is that it is a bit more conversational and gives in gracefully on the extra problem, which suits this student's impatience. Y was slightly shorter, 9 minutes against 11, despite doing more. Both kept a student at risk of leaving in the chat.

Z beats X even though X scored higher on the test. X's extra point is recall of a rule from his notes, which he then fails to apply on Q1 and Q2. Neither session repaired anything. But X also set up copying all 10 questions, so he gets no practice at all and is more dependent on the bot. Z at least practised the sign-change step correctly and handed in honest work. A teacher marking it would see the error and could correct it. X hides the error from the teacher behind correct copied answers.

## Tutor behaviour critique

**Session W tutor**
- Helped: a two-sentence refusal that names his goal ("you'll fly through the other nine"). Sending him back to the line in capitals in his notes ([3]) instead of re-explaining. A clear yes/no on −7 when he pushed back, followed by a very narrow question ("What undoes taking away 12?"). Asking him to explain why it isn't 5 − 12 ([7]). An optional transfer problem.
- Hurt: at [8] the tutor said "The sign flips when it crosses the equals sign." That endorses the move-and-flip shortcut behind his sign misconception. It should have said: "Right, you add 12 to *both sides*. That's why it looks like it flips." The practice problem 2(x − 3) = x + 4 has the same shape as the original. It should have stretched him with a negative x-term inside the bracket, such as 2(5 − x) = x − 2, which is where the bracket error usually comes back (and it is what the test asked).
- Concrete change: when he said "is it -7 or not can u just say", the tutor could have said "Put −7 back into 3(x − 4) and 2x + 5. Do they match?" That answers his yes/no question through his own work and gives him a check he can use on the other nine. Before he left, add one line: "Check each answer by putting it back in."

**Session X tutor (answer bot)**
- Helped: the worked solution is clear and correct, and it includes a substitution check that could have been useful if he had read it.
- Hurt: it gave the whole solution to a student who asked for nothing else, and then agreed to do all 9 remaining questions ("Paste the remaining questions and I'll work through each one for you"). That guarantees no practice and no repair of either misconception, and the test shows both errors untouched.
- Concrete changes: at [0], ask "What do you get when you expand 3(x − 4)?" before showing anything. At least, show the method for Q7 and then require him to try Q8 before helping further. When he said "can u just do them too", it should have offered to check his answers instead of doing them.

**Session Y tutor**
- Helped: an up-front time limit and deal ([0]), which suits a low-motivation student. Substitution tests that show him his errors with his own arithmetic ([1], [5]). Not saying "wrong" and letting the numbers say it. Asking him to name both errors ([8]). The solo next question with a light "how could you check it?" at the end.
- Hurt or risky: the substitution tests mean extra arithmetic for an impatient student, and three rounds (x = 5, x = −7, x = 17) is close to the limit. x = 5 makes 3(x − 4) small and easy, which was good, but the x = −7 round with negatives was the slowest moment and could have triggered a walkout. At [3] the tutor stacked two requests in one turn (check x = 5 again, then the next step), which is fine for this student but could confuse a weaker one. The session ended after Q8 without a word about the bracket shape with x as a negative second term.
- Concrete change: after he named his two errors at [9], the tutor could make the solo question one with x second and negative inside the bracket (e.g. "try one like 3(4 − x) = x"), or at least say "watch the sign when the x is the second thing in the bracket". When he said "can i go now", the closing line was right. It could add "if a check doesn't match, look at your bracket and your signs first". That turns his two named errors into a checklist.
