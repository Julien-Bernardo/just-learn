## Comparison
Ranking for Sam's learning: **X > W > Y > Z.**

- **X vs W.** Both tutors were Socratic, made Sam trace before running, and left him doing about 75–80% of the reasoning. The differences that made X better:
  1. **Intervening at the moment of failure.** When Sam's None version failed and he wrote "wtf", X said "Don't change anything yet. Trace [7] by hand." That breaks the guess-and-check reflex at the exact moment it would fire. W did similar things ("predict before you run"), but its biggest correction came when Sam asked to be told the answer. There the tutor held firm with a hint, which was right, but it was a standoff rather than a habit repair.
  2. **Self-explanation of every bug** at [24] consolidated the lesson. W's one-sentence summary was thinner.
  3. **A near-transfer task with a well-chosen test input** ([3,-2,-2,9]), where the student made a mistake and caught it by tracing. W's twist (what if duplicates counted) was good for checking understanding but did not produce new code. It also left the "duplicates of the min" idea unpractised, which shows up as W's Q2 mislabel.
  4. **Pacing.** X took 30 minutes against W's 49, partly because X endorsed None as also solving [7]. W let Sam go through 0 → None → TypeError → -inf, which was instructive (the counterexample for 0 was excellent) but slower under deadline pressure.

  W's advantage is that it made Sam consider a bad sentinel value (0) and test it against negatives, which builds edge-case intuition. X's student only got that from the notes. On balance X delivered similar depth more efficiently and with transfer practice.
- **Y** shows what the notes alone can do. The tracing advice worked when used, but without anyone to prompt him, Sam reverted to patching and finished with `min(nums)` that he does not understand. The test score is decent because he wrote the code himself, but the habit change is shallow and he was close to bypassing to an answer bot.
- **Z** produced the fastest correct submission and the least learning. The marked 83% overstates what this session supports.

## Tutor behaviour critique
**Session W**
- Helped:
  - Asked what Sam had tried before anything else ([0]), which surfaced the guess-and-check habit.
  - Prediction before running at [10] and [26].
  - At [16], the counterexample [-3,-5,-8] to refute "0", instead of saying it was wrong.
  - Turned the TypeError into a reasoning prompt ([24]).
  - Pushed "don't trust 'i think'" at [46], toward verifying by tracing.
- Hurt or missed:
  - **Pacing.** 49 minutes, under a 2-hour deadline, after the student explicitly signalled time pressure.
  - **The None/-inf choice.** When Sam offered "None maybe? or -infinity" at [21], the tutor should have asked "which of those already gives you the right answer for [7]?". That would have saved the later -inf → None patch, connected the starting value to the spec, and avoided the -inf sentinel flaw.
  - **Thin edge-case prompt.** At [37], "name two or three inputs" was too open. The tutor should have pointed him to the notes' list and asked what each case tests. In particular, the "answer appears first" and "duplicates of the second-place value vs the first-place value" distinctions were never made explicit, which led to his Q2 mislabel.
- Change: replace or add to the twist question with a short `second_smallest` near-transfer task traced on a list containing a duplicate of the min.

**Session X**
- Helped:
  - Framed tracing as faster than guessing, which suited a deadline-driven student ([2]).
  - "Don't change anything yet. Trace [7]" at [14].
  - Pointed to the notes rather than listing the edge cases itself ([19]).
  - Asked for a full bug post-mortem in his own words ([24]).
  - Chose a transfer input that covers negatives, a duplicated min and the answer appearing first.
  - "Before I say yes or no, trace it" at [27]. This withholds approval so the student owns verification.
- Hurt or missed:
  - **Misleading endorsement.** At [11], "None also matches what the task wants for [7], so it's a good pick" was slightly misleading, because with `largest = nums[0]` it did not fix [7] by itself. That probably caused the "wtf". It worked out as a productive failure, but it was luck rather than design.
  - **The 10-minute estimate** at [0] was off by a factor of 3. That is a trust risk with a deadline-anxious student.
- Change: after the practice task, ask "what is each number in [3,-2,-2,9] there to test?" so Sam builds his own test-design reasoning rather than receiving a good input.

**Session Z**
- Hurt:
  - Delivered the diagnosis, the full fix and an alternative in the first message, without asking what Sam had tried or getting him to trace anything. That fits a student whose explicit weakness is not tracing.
  - The tracing tip and the offer to check his trace came after he already had working code, so he had no reason to take them up.
  - "Nice work!" at [13] praised copying.
- Change: when Sam pasted the failures, the tutor should have done what X did: ask for a hand-trace of [5,1,4] on his own code. If time pressure demanded speed, it could have named the bug category ("second never updates for values between second and largest") and let him write the fix. Even after giving code, it should have asked him to trace [5,5,3] on the new version before submitting, not after.
